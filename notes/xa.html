Investment Adviser – Level 1

Learn to think like a fee-only fiduciary: plan the money, pick the mix, measure the result, follow the rules.

Think of an Investment Adviser as a family doctor for money: before prescribing anything, the doctor checks your budget, your debts, your goals and how much risk you can stomach, and is paid by you, not by the pharmacy. X-A is the first of two NISM exams (with X-B) that every individual SEBI-registered Investment Adviser, every principal officer of a non-individual adviser and every person associated with advice must pass. It covers personal financial planning and time value of money, India's markets and products (equity, bonds, derivatives, mutual funds, PMS, AIFs), portfolio theory and performance measurement, and the rules, ethics and grievance systems an adviser lives by. SEBI requires it so that anyone charging a fee for advice meets a common minimum knowledge benchmark and acts in the client's best interest.

Introduction to Personal Financial Planning

Financial planning lines up a household's income, expenses, assets and liabilities so that today's needs and tomorrow's goals are both funded. It is holistic, goal-centred and continuous, unlike one-off product advice.

Make it stickVinod, 40, earns ₹2 lakh a month and saves ₹40,000. He thinks he is fine. A planner asks: emergency fund? enough life and health cover? how big a corpus for his child's college, and is ₹40,000 enough to build it? Financial planning is just asking those questions in order.
  1. Financial planning = streamlining income, expenses, assets and liabilities to meet current and future needs; it identifies goals and puts an action plan in place.
  2. It differs from typical advisory: comprehensive (retirement, insurance, investment, estate), goal-centred, coordinated, continuously monitored and considers risk as well as return.
  3. Goals must be specific, measurable, realistic and time-bound, and then ranked by priority (education and retirement before luxury spending).
  4. Some goals may be staggered (pushed back) rather than cancelled; time-bound goals like a child's education can be bridged with prudent loans.
  5. Scope: goal setting, cash flow and budgeting, insurance planning, debt management, investment planning and asset allocation, tax planning, retirement planning, estate planning.
  6. Advisers take a top-down asset-allocation approach rather than focusing on stock or bond picking.
  7. Tax efficiency should not be the basis of investment decisions; requirements and risk appetite should be.
  8. Physical assets (real estate, gold) are tangible, inflation hedges but illiquid and lightly regulated; financial assets are standardised, regulated, divisible and easier to compare.
  9. Net worth = Assets - Liabilities; loans used to buy financial assets (leveraging) are risky because of price volatility.
  10. Six-step process: establish relationship, gather data and goals, analyse status, develop and present recommendations, implement, monitor.
  11. Business models: fee-only planners, execution-only (distributors earning commission), wraps and platforms.
  12. An individual IA cannot do both advisory and distribution; a non-individual IA needs client-level segregation at group level and an arm's-length separate division.
  13. IAs may offer execution only through direct schemes/products, with no consideration at group or family level.

Time Value of Money

Money today is worth more than the same money later because it can earn a return. Present value, future value, CAGR, EMI, NPER, annuities and perpetuities are the tools that move cash flows across time.

Make it stick₹100 in a 6% one-month deposit becomes ₹100.50. So ₹100 today equals ₹100.50 next month; if someone offers you just ₹100 next month, you are losing 50 paise. Every TVM problem is that trade-off, scaled up.
  1. TVM: earlier cash flows are worth more; the later the cash flow, the lower its PV; the higher the discount rate, the lower the PV.
  2. PV = FV / (1+r)^n; FV = PV x (1+r)^n, where r and n are per compounding period.
  3. Adjust for compounding frequency: 8% p.a. compounded quarterly means 2% per quarter and 4 periods a year.
  4. More frequent compounding gives higher maturity value (₹5 lakh at 8% for 5 years: ₹7,34,664 annual vs ₹7,42,974 quarterly).
  5. Simple interest ignores compounding: interest taken out and spent earns nothing further.
  6. CAGR = (End/Begin)^(1/n) - 1; for days use n = days/365, so the exponent becomes 365/days.
  7. Excel: PV(rate,nper,pmt,fv,type), FV(rate,nper,pmt,pv,type), PMT, NPER, RATE; type 0 = end of period, 1 = beginning.
  8. EMI uses monthly rate (annual/12) and months as periods; ₹30 lakh, 6.5%, 20 years gives EMI of about ₹22,367.
  9. Annuity: regular payments; fixed vs floating annuities. Ordinary annuity pays at period end; annuity due at the start and is worth more.
  10. PV of ordinary annuity = C x [1 - 1/(1+r)^n] / r; ₹5,000 for 4 years at 10% = ₹15,849 (ordinary) vs ₹17,434 (due).
  11. Perpetuity PV = C / r (e.g. ₹10,000 at 8% = ₹1,25,000); perpetual bonds and lifelong pensions are examples.
  12. Annuity tables give a factor: PV = payment x factor (10 years at 5% = 7.7217).

Evaluating the Financial Position of Clients

Budgets, income-expenditure statements and a personal balance sheet tell you where the client stands; personal finance ratios turn that picture into numbers you can track.

Make it stickNFG spends ₹1.5 lakh a month and holds ₹7 lakh in savings, ₹2 lakh in short FDs and ₹4 lakh in liquid funds. Liquid assets ₹13 lakh / ₹1.5 lakh = 8.66 months of cover: comfortable. The ₹3 lakh in shares does not count because its value can swing.
  1. Cash flow management matters because timing mismatches force costly borrowing even when income exceeds spending.
  2. Budget order: regular income, less mandatory expenses (loans, taxes) = disposable income, less essential living expenses, less discretionary expenses = savings.
  3. Savings include PF contributions (employer and employee), even though not received in hand.
  4. Income-expenditure statement is accrual-based (salary for the month counted even if received next day); a cash statement is not.
  5. Emergency fund should be the first goal: six months of expenses in liquid assets; can be laddered with about three months liquid if income is secure; review annually or when a big EMI is added.
  6. Savings ratio = Savings / Income; Expense ratio = Recurring expenses / Income = 1 - Savings ratio.
  7. Savings-to-income ratio (accumulated savings / annual income) should be at least 3 times in the early 40s; self-occupied home excluded.
  8. Leverage ratio = Total liabilities / Total assets; Solvency ratio = Net worth / Total assets = 1 - Leverage ratio.
  9. Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets / Monthly expenses (including EMIs); 4 to 6 is comfortable; shares, long FDs and closed-end funds are excluded.
  10. Liquid assets: savings bank, FDs maturing within 6 months, liquid funds.
  11. Financial assets ratio = Financial assets / Total assets; higher is preferred as goals approach.
  12. Debt-to-income ratio = Monthly debt servicing / Monthly income; above 35-40% is excessive.
  13. Non-recurring expenses are excluded from the expense ratio; periodic items are annualised (a ₹6 lakh holiday every 3 years = ₹2 lakh a year).
  14. Conservative asset estimate excludes personal jewellery, the self-occupied house and personal-use assets like a car.

Debt Management and Loans

Borrowing uses tomorrow's income today. Advisers help clients borrow for the right purpose, at the right cost and tenor, understand loan maths, and get out of debt faster.

Make it stickRevolving a credit card at 3% a month feels small, but (1.03)^12 - 1 is a 42.6% annual cost. That single line explains why every repayment plan in this chapter kills credit-card debt first.
  1. Borrow ideally for appreciating assets or income-raising expenses (e.g. education); never for regular expenses; margin-funded investing magnifies losses.
  2. Secured loans (home, vehicle, gold, against securities) are cheaper; unsecured (personal loans, credit cards) are expensive.
  3. Shorter tenor = higher EMI pressure; longer tenor = more total interest.
  4. Credit score (CIBIL range 300-900) is built by timely payment, low utilisation and a mix of secured and unsecured credit; one free full credit report per calendar year from each credit bureau.
  5. Credit bureaus are licensed by RBI under the Credit Information Companies (Regulation) Act, 2005: CIBIL, Experian, CRIF High Mark, Equifax.
  6. Pledge: movable asset stays with lender; hypothecation: asset stays with borrower (car loan); mortgage: immovable property.
  7. Hire purchase transfers ownership on the last instalment; lease never transfers ownership to the lessee.
  8. Moratorium pauses EMIs without default but interest keeps accruing; pre-EMI is interest-only until full disbursal.
  9. RBI: no prepayment charges on home loans from banks.
  10. Education loans repay after course completion or employment, whichever is earlier, usually over 5-8 years; vehicle loans 3-7 years.
  11. In an EMI, the interest share is highest at the start; Excel IPMT gives interest and PPMT the principal for a given period.
  12. Restructuring should be judged by the present value of new payments; a haircut lowers PV.
  13. Most borrowers prefer to keep EMI fixed and change tenure when floating rates change.
  14. Avalanche = highest rate first; Snowball = smallest balance first (motivational); Blizzard = smallest first, then switch to highest rate.

Introduction to the Indian Financial Markets

Who regulates what in India, and how banks, NBFCs, money markets, forex, credit bureaus, account aggregators and the SEBI-regulated securities market fit together.

Make it stickPicture India's financial system as a city: RBI runs the water and power (money, banks, payments), SEBI polices the stock bazaar, IRDAI looks after insurance, PFRDA after pensions, and the Finance Ministry is the municipal council over them all.
  1. Ministry of Finance has five departments: Economic Affairs (capital markets, macro policy), Expenditure, Revenue (CBDT and CBEC), Financial Services (PSU banks, insurance, pension reforms), DIPAM (disinvestment).
  2. Ministry of Corporate Affairs administers the Companies Act 2013, LLP Act 2008 and Competition Act 2002, and oversees ICAI, ICSI and ICMAI; RoC registers companies.
  3. RBI: monetary policy (price stability with growth), regulator of banks and payment systems, forex manager, currency issuer, banker to government and banks.
  4. SEBI (statutory body under SEBI Act, 1992): protect investors, develop and regulate the securities market.
  5. IRDAI (IRDA Act, 1999) licenses insurers and regulates products and intermediaries; PFRDA (PFRDA Act, 2013) regulates NPS.
  6. SRO under SEBI (SRO) Regulations, 2004: organisation of intermediaries recognised by SEBI, excluding a stock exchange.
  7. Payments banks: deposits up to ₹1,00,000, debit cards but no credit cards, no lending.
  8. NBFCs cannot accept demand deposits, issue cheques or offer DICGC deposit insurance; HFCs now regulated by RBI.
  9. Money market: maturity under 1 year; call (overnight), notice (up to 14 days), CP, CD, T-bills.
  10. FBIL publishes daily reference rates for USD, EUR, GBP and JPY against INR.
  11. Account Aggregators are RBI-licensed and share data only with the customer's consent, which can be revoked anytime.
  12. Securities are defined in Section 2(h) of SCRA, 1956; ULIPs are excluded.
  13. Depositories: NSDL and CDSL; DPs are agents of depositories; custodians serve institutions.

Securities Market Segments

Primary markets raise new capital (IPO, FPO, private placement, QIP, rights); secondary markets give liquidity and price discovery, protected by margins, circuit breakers and settlement guarantees. Corporate actions change the shareholder's position.

Make it stickIn a book-built issue of 5,000 shares, bids pile up from the top price down: 1,000 at ₹144, 1,500 at ₹140, 2,500 at ₹135. The book fills at ₹135, so that is the cut-off and everyone who bid ₹135 or more is allotted at ₹135.
  1. IPO is by an unlisted issuer; FPO by a listed issuer; both can include an offer for sale (OFS) by existing holders, whose proceeds go to the sellers, not the company.
  2. Preferential issue: listed issuer to select persons; QIP: listed issuer to qualified institutional buyers.
  3. Book building: price band ceiling up to 20% above the floor; cut-off is the price at which the issue is subscribed.
  4. Retail discount to cut-off cannot exceed 10%.
  5. Minimum application value for book-built issues: ₹10,000 to ₹15,000; applications only through ASBA (UPI for retail).
  6. Public debt issues need at least one credit rating (all ratings disclosed) and a SEBI-registered debenture trustee.
  7. T-bills (91, 182, 364 days) are issued only by the Central Government; State Governments issue only dated securities (SGS).
  8. Market-wide circuit breakers at 10%, 15% and 20% on Sensex or Nifty 50, whichever is breached first.
  9. Price bands: 2%, 5% or 10% on specified scrips; 20% on remaining scrips; none for derivatives scrips (operating range 10%).
  10. Clearing corporation becomes counterparty to every trade (novation); Core SGF guarantees settlement.
  11. Stock exchanges inspect trading members' books at least once a year.
  12. Bonus issue comes from free reserves; not allowed if the company has defaulted on debt or FD payments.
  13. Stock split 1:5 turns 100 shares of ₹10 face value into 500 shares of ₹2; holding value unchanged.
  14. Buyback extinguishes shares and raises EPS; delisting can be voluntary (reverse book building) or compulsory.

Introduction to Investment

A tour of asset classes: equity and preference shares, debt and credit ratings, commodities, real estate, structured products, distressed securities, art, overseas investing, and the channels to access them.

Make it stickTata Motors issued DVR shares in 2008 with 1/10th the voting rights but a 5% higher dividend: you trade a slice of control for a slice more cash. Every asset in this chapter is a similar trade-off of risk, return, liquidity and control.
  1. Preference shares rank above equity for dividends and liquidation; usually no voting rights; can be cumulative/non-cumulative, participating/non-participating, convertible; called hybrid securities.
  2. DVRs permitted in India since 2000 with limited adoption.
  3. Credit spread = corporate yield - G-Sec yield of same maturity; it is the risk premium for default.
  4. Rating scale AAA (highest safety) to D (default); BBB and above is investment grade, below BBB is high yield or junk.
  5. Soft commodities (grown, perishable, volatile, weather-driven) vs hard commodities (mined); gold is a safe-haven asset; commodities give no current income.
  6. Real estate: inflation hedge, diversification, long-term, large ticket; REITs give smaller-ticket access.
  7. Structured products use derivatives; protection can be 0% to 100%, not guaranteed; Market Linked Debentures are the common form.
  8. Distressed securities ('fallen angels') need special valuation skill; popular with hedge funds, PMS and AIF products.
  9. LRS lets a resident individual invest up to USD 2,50,000 abroad per financial year.
  10. Managed portfolios: Mutual Funds, AIFs, Portfolio Managers, Collective Investment Schemes; all SEBI-regulated.
  11. Minimum ticket: AIF ₹1 crore, PMS ₹50 lakh; MFs are the most stringently regulated because they serve retail investors.

Investing in Stocks

Equity is the residual claim with the highest risk and expected return. Diversify across sectors and time, research top-down or bottom-up, value with DCF, asset-based or relative multiples, and time with technical analysis.

Make it stickTwo stocks: one at ₹10 with P/E 75, another at ₹100 with P/E 20. The ₹10 stock is the 'expensive' one, because you pay ₹75 for each rupee of earnings. Price tells you nothing; multiples tell you value.
  1. Equity holders have the last (residual) claim on assets.
  2. Cross-sectional diversification = across sectors and geographies at a time; time diversification = staying invested long ('time in the market').
  3. Market (systematic) risk is measured by beta, cannot be diversified but can be hedged; sector and company-specific risks can be diversified.
  4. Liquidity risk is measured by impact cost; lower impact cost = more liquid.
  5. Sell-side analysts publish recommendations for clients; buy-side analysts work for fund managers for internal use.
  6. EIC framework: Economy, Industry, Company; top-down starts with the economy, bottom-up with the company.
  7. Industry life cycle: introduction, growth, maturity (longest), decline; defensives (FMCG, pharma) outperform in recessions; cyclicals in early recovery.
  8. Price is a fact, value is an opinion; three valuation approaches: DCF, asset-based, relative valuation.
  9. P/E = Price / EPS; trailing uses last four quarters, forward uses next four estimated quarters; earnings yield = EPS / Price = inverse of P/E.
  10. PEG = P/E / EPS growth; below 1 suggests undervalued, above 1 overvalued.
  11. P/BV popular for banks and financial services; P/S useful for loss-making or low-margin firms; EV = Market cap + Debt - Excess cash.
  12. EV/EBITDA suits capital-intensive firms with no PAT; EVA = NOPAT - (Invested capital x Cost of capital); MVA = Market value - capital contributed.
  13. Dividend yield = DPS / Market price x 100; rising price lowers yield.
  14. Technical analysis: prices move in trends; tools include trend lines, moving averages (5 to 200 days), Bollinger bands (2 standard deviations).

Investing in Fixed Income Securities

Bonds are priced as the present value of coupons and principal at the market yield. Price and yield move inversely; duration measures sensitivity. The chapter also maps the money market, G-Sec market, corporate bond market and small savings schemes.

Make it stickA 10% coupon, 5-year bond when the market yields 8% is worth ₹107.99, not ₹100, because it pays more than the market. Pay a premium today and the bond is 'pulled to par' at ₹100 by maturity.
  1. Risks: interest-rate, call, reinvestment, credit (downgrade, spread, default), liquidity, exchange-rate, inflation, volatility, political/legal, event.
  2. Holding to maturity removes interest-rate (price) risk; zero-coupon bonds and STRIPS have no reinvestment risk.
  3. Bond price = sum of PV of cash flows at YTM; semi-annual payments are worth slightly more than annual for the same coupon and yield.
  4. Par: G-Sec face value ₹100; corporate bonds typically ₹10,000 (as per workbook); quotes are a % of face value.
  5. Coupon yield = coupon / face value; current yield = coupon / market price; YTM is the IRR of the bond.
  6. At par CR = CY = YTM; at discount CR < CY < YTM; at premium CR > CY > YTM.
  7. Longer maturity and lower coupon mean greater price sensitivity; the price-yield curve is convex.
  8. Dirty price = clean price + accrued interest; markets quote clean price.
  9. Day count: bonds 30/360; money market actual/365.
  10. Perpetual bond value = Coupon / Yield (8 on 100 at 6% = ₹133.33).
  11. Macaulay duration is always less than or equal to maturity; equals maturity only for a zero-coupon bond; modified duration = Macaulay / (1 + yield).
  12. Yield curve shapes: normal (upward), inverted (signals recession), flat, humped.
  13. Money market: call (overnight), notice (2-14 days), term (15 days-1 year), CP (7 days-1 year, ₹5 lakh multiples), CD (banks 7 days-1 year, FIs 1-3 years, ₹1 lakh multiples), CMBs under 91 days, T-bills 91/182/364 days.
  14. Debenture trustee is compulsory for debentures with maturity over 18 months; DICGC insures bank deposits up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank.
  15. Small savings: PPF 15 years, NSC 5 years (min ₹1,000), POMIS max ₹9 lakh single / ₹15 lakh joint, SCSS for 60+ (55 for retirees within 3 months), Sukanya Samriddhi (girl under 10, ₹250 to ₹1.5 lakh a year, matures at 21 years).

Understanding Derivatives

Derivatives derive value from an underlying. Forwards are private OTC deals; futures are standardised exchange contracts; options give a right without an obligation; swaps exchange cash flows. They are used to hedge, speculate and arbitrage.

Make it stickBuy a stock at ₹3,984 in cash and sell its futures at ₹4,032 for 27 days: you lock in ₹48, about 16.3% annualised, because spot and futures must converge on expiry. That is the engine inside every arbitrage fund.
  1. SCRA includes derivatives in the definition of securities; RBI Act section 45U(a) also defines derivatives.
  2. Derivatives are a zero-sum game before taxes and transaction costs.
  3. SEBI mandates physical settlement for stock derivatives; index derivatives are cash settled.
  4. Initial margin = SPAN margin + extreme loss margin; should cover losses in 99% of cases; option buyers pay premium margin.
  5. Open interest = outstanding contracts not yet settled; it is not trading volume.
  6. Forwards: bilateral, customised, OTC; suffer liquidity and counterparty risk.
  7. Futures: standardised, exchange-traded, clearing house guarantees, both sides pay margin.
  8. Options: buyer has right, seller (writer) has obligation; call = right to buy, put = right to sell; buyer's loss limited to premium; writer's loss potentially unlimited.
  9. Option premium = intrinsic value + time value; in-the-money options have positive intrinsic value.
  10. Swaps: interest rate and currency swaps; notional principal is never exchanged in an interest rate swap.
  11. Futures price = Spot + cost of carry; basis = futures - spot; spot and futures converge on expiry.
  12. Commodity derivatives traded on MCX, NCDEX, ICEX, NSE and BSE under SEBI; categories: bullion, metals, energy, agriculture.
  13. Currency futures on USD, EUR, GBP, JPY vs INR plus cross pairs EUR-USD, GBP-USD, USD-JPY.
  14. Derivatives trading started in India in June 2000; most Indian options are European.

Mutual Funds

A mutual fund pools money into a portfolio owned by unitholders. Know the structure, NAV maths, open vs closed vs interval vs ETF, SEBI categories with their allocation limits, investment options, systematic plans and direct vs regular plans.

Make it stickA fund has net assets of ₹1,00,000 and 10,000 units: NAV ₹10. If an investor were wrongly allotted at ₹10 when the NAV is ₹11.11, every other unitholder's NAV drops to ₹11. That is why every purchase and redemption must happen at the applicable NAV.
  1. Sponsor sets up the fund; trustees protect investors; AMC manages schemes; R&T agent keeps investor records; SID and KIM on AMC website.
  2. NAV = Net assets / Units outstanding; purchases and redemptions at NAV do not change NAV; portfolio is marked to market daily.
  3. Closed-end funds must be listed; interval funds open for at least 2 days with a minimum 15-day gap between windows and are also listed.
  4. Only one scheme per category per fund house, except index funds/ETFs, FoFs and sectoral/thematic funds.
  5. Large cap = top 100, mid cap = 101-250, small cap = 251 onwards (AMFI list, full market cap).
  6. Large cap at least 80%; mid cap 65%; small cap 65%; large and mid cap 35% + 35%; multi cap 75% equity with 25% each in large, mid, small; flexi cap 65% equity.
  7. Sector and thematic funds at least 80%; value, contra, dividend yield 65% equity; an AMC can have value OR contra, not both; focussed funds max 30 stocks.
  8. ELSS: at least 80% equity, 3-year lock-in, deduction up to ₹1,50,000 under section 123 (80C) in the old regime.

PMS offers individually managed portfolios to HNIs under the SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020: discretionary, non-discretionary or advisory, with fixed and performance fees governed by hurdle rates and high-water marks.

Introduction to Modern Portfolio Theory

Markowitz showed that portfolio risk depends on weights, individual volatilities and correlations. Combining imperfectly correlated assets reduces risk; the efficient frontier holds the best risk-return combinations.

Make it stickTwo stocks each with 5% standard deviation and 0.5 correlation, held 50:50, give a portfolio standard deviation of only 4.33%, not 5%. The missing 0.67% is the free lunch of diversification.
  1. MPT: Harry Markowitz, 'Portfolio Selection', Journal of Finance, 1952; Nobel Prize 1990.
  2. Assumptions: investors maximise return for given risk, maximise one-period expected utility, have diminishing marginal utility of wealth, and judge on expected return and variance only.
  3. Risk averse investors reject a fair game (zero risk premium); risk neutral judge only expected return; risk seekers accept fair games.
  4. Certainty equivalent rate: risk-free rate that is as attractive as the risky portfolio.
  5. Expected return = sum of probability x return; portfolio expected return = weighted average of asset returns.
  6. Variance from sample uses n - 1 in the denominator; standard deviation = square root of variance.
  7. Two-asset variance = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2w1w2ρσ1σ2.
  8. Number of covariance terms = (n² - n)/2; 50 securities need 1,225.
  9. With perfect positive correlation there is no diversification benefit; the risk-return line is straight.
  10. Efficient frontier: highest return for each risk level; portfolios below it are sub-optimal.
  11. Optimisation needs expected returns, standard deviations and correlations; errors in these create estimation risk.

Portfolio Construction Process

Build portfolios from an Investment Policy Statement: objectives, constraints, personality, life-cycle stage and capital-market forecasts lead to strategic asset allocation, tactical tilts and disciplined rebalancing.

Make it stickBB&K would call the entrepreneur who bets big an Adventurer, the trend-chaser a Celebrity, the research-driven contrarian an Individualist, the retiree protecting wealth a Guardian, and the balanced middle a Straight Arrow. Each needs a different conversation.
  1. Asset allocation is the most important investment decision: 40% of variation in returns across funds and 90% of a single fund's variation over time (Brinson, Hood and Beebower 1986; Ibbotson and Kaplan 2000).
  2. Correlation within the same asset class is high; across classes it is low; correlations change over time.
  3. Process: policy statement, study conditions and forecasts, construct, measure and evaluate, periodic review.
  4. IPS sets realistic expectations, guides the manager, gives an evaluation framework and protects the investor from inappropriate or unethical decisions.
  5. Objectives: capital preservation, regular income, capital appreciation; risk and return move together, liquidity and return move inversely.
  6. Liquidity needs: emergency cash (usually 2-3 months' spending in this chapter), near-term goals, investment flexibility.
  7. Constraints: liquidity, regulatory (LRS USD 2,50,000; insider trading), tax, time horizon, unique preferences (ESG, ethical).
  8. Sustainable investing uses ESG criteria; ethical investing is more personal and avoids 'sin' sectors.
  9. BB&K uses two axes: confident vs anxious and careful vs impetuous; five types: Adventurer, Celebrity, Individualist, Guardian, Straight Arrow.
  10. Life cycle: accumulation, consolidation, decumulation/spending, gifting.
  11. Benchmark should match the portfolio (large cap vs Nifty 50, mid cap vs mid-cap index) and be reviewed.
  12. SAA = long-term policy mix ('time in the market'); TAA = short-term tilts ('timing the market').
  13. Rebalancing trades off transaction and tax costs against drift from target allocation.

Portfolio Performance Measurement and Evaluation

Measure returns properly (HPR, TWRR, MWRR, geometric vs arithmetic, net, post-tax, cash-drag adjusted), measure risk (standard deviation, beta, tracking error) and judge skill with risk-adjusted ratios, benchmarks and attribution.

Make it stickManager A turns ₹100 into ₹50 and back to ₹100: arithmetic average return 25%, actual wealth gain zero. Manager B earns 10% twice: ₹121. Geometric return exposes A instantly, which is why long-term comparisons use it.
  1. HPR = (Income + End - Begin) / Begin.
  2. MWRR = IRR of the investor's cash flows (depends on timing); TWRR chains sub-period returns and removes the effect of cash flows; TWRR equals geometric return.
  3. SEBI PM Regulations 2020 require discretionary portfolio managers to disclose performance using TWRR.
  4. Arithmetic mean is the best estimate of a single future year; geometric mean is right for multi-year compounding and is always lower unless all returns are equal.
  5. Net return deducts expenses, management fee, performance fee and exit load from gross return.
  6. Post-tax return = Pre-tax return x (1 - tax rate).
  7. Cash-drag adjusted return reports on the whole capital, including uninvested cash.
  8. CAPM required return = Rf + β(Rm - Rf); Jensen alpha = actual return - CAPM required return.
  9. Portfolio beta is the weighted average of individual betas; beta above 1 = more volatile than market.
  10. Tracking error = standard deviation of (portfolio - benchmark) returns; tracking difference = simple return gap.
  11. Sharpe = (Rp - Rf)/σp, uses total risk; Treynor = (Rp - Rf)/βp, uses systematic risk; Sortino uses downside deviation; Information ratio = active return / tracking error.
  12. Use Sharpe for an investor who is not well diversified; Treynor for a well-diversified investor; identical rankings for fully diversified portfolios.
  13. M² levers or delevers the portfolio to market volatility and compares returns directly.
  14. Good benchmark: defined constituents, investable, consistent with style, same risk profile, measurable.
  15. Attribution: allocation effect, selection effect; studies show market timing rarely explains outperformance; Fama's net selectivity is an absolute measure.
  16. Rupee appreciation reduces returns on foreign investments; rupee depreciation adds to them.

Ethical Issues

Ethics goes beyond legality. An IA is a fiduciary who must put the client first, disclose conflicts, give suitable advice with a reasonable basis and resolve ethical dilemmas with principle.

Make it stickA relationship manager is told to push ULIPs because they pay more, but personally believes pure term cover suits most clients. That clash between personal and professional values is a classic ethical dilemma the chapter wants you to recognise.
  1. Ethics = moral principles guiding conduct towards others (from Greek 'ethos'); it goes beyond legal compliance.
  2. Client First principle: trust takes years to build and moments to lose.
  3. Grievance areas: not understanding client needs, not understanding products, not knowing the wider market, churning for commission, hiding risks, poor after-sales service.
  4. Examples: small-cap funds for senior citizens, arbitrage funds sold as liquid-fund substitutes, distress debt AIFs without explaining default risk.
  5. Ethical dilemma: choice between alternatives where neither is clearly superior; a pure dilemma is where every option breaches some principle.
  6. Resolve by deeper analysis, principles (most good or least harm) or reframing the problem.
  7. Fiduciary duty rests on utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei): full disclosure, suitable advice, reasonable basis, proper execution without conflict.
  8. SEBI Do's: deal only with registered IAs, verify registration, pay fees only through banking channels, insist on risk profiling, get terms in writing, report assured-return offers.
  9. SEBI Don'ts: no stock tips, never give money for investment to the IA, no assured returns, no luring ads, no limited-period discounts, no decisions on calls alone.
  10. Annual compliance audit by a practising Chartered Accountant (per this chapter); material observations disclosed to clients.
  11. Global practice: US SEC requires disclosure of financial condition impairing obligations and material disciplinary events of the past 10 years.

Grievance Redress Mechanism

Complain first to the provider, then to the regulator's system (SCORES, RBI Integrated Ombudsman, IRDAI IGMS and Insurance Ombudsman, NPS Trust), then ODR, SAT, courts or consumer forums.

Make it stickA client unhappy with her IA writes to the IA first. Still unhappy, she files on SCORES. Still unhappy, she goes to Smart ODR for conciliation or arbitration. Three doors, in that order.
  1. Consumer Protection Act, 1986: a consumer buys goods or hires services for consideration; excludes purchase for resale or commercial purpose.
  2. A robust system records source, date and time, nature, internal vs external resolution, status (ATR), ageing and escalation.
  3. IA offices must display details of the compliance officer and CEO/partner/proprietor.
  4. Capital market path: market participant, then SCORES, then ODR portal (which can also be used at any stage after the participant fails to resolve).
  5. SCORES does not take incomplete complaints, suggestions, complaints about trading price or illiquidity, or private agreement disputes; SEBI does not handle complaints against unlisted/delisted/wound-up companies, sub-judice matters or other regulators' areas.
  6. Banking: three-tier internal (Branch Manager, Zonal Manager, GM-Customer Service); Integrated Ombudsman if no satisfactory reply within one month; ombudsman passes an award if no settlement within one month.
  7. BCSBI was set up by RBI as an independent watchdog of fair treatment; CPGRAMS (pgportal.gov.in) handles public grievances.
  8. Insurance: insurer first, IGMS, then Insurance Ombudsman (appointed by Government of India) for claim repudiation, premium disputes, delays, non-issue of documents; recommendation within one month, award within 3 months; acceptance within one month.
  9. NPS: CRA (NSDL e-Gov), then NPS Trust if unresolved in 30 days, then Ombudsman.
  10. SAT (sections 15T/15U SEBI Act): appeal within 45 days against SEBI, adjudicating officer, IRDAI or PFRDA orders; has civil court powers; appeal to Supreme Court within 60 days.
  11. NBFC deposit non-repayment: NCLT or consumer forum; unlisted company bonds/deposits: Ministry of Corporate Affairs.

📖 Glossary

FiduciarySomeone who must act in the client's best interest with utmost good faith; SEBI requires IAs to act in a fiduciary capacity.
Part-time investment adviserAn IA that also runs a non-securities business or employment; capped at 75 clients.
Persons associated with investment advicePartners, officers, employees or sales staff of an IA who give advice to its clients.
Principal officerThe senior person (MD, designated director, managing partner or business head) responsible for a non-individual IA's advisory business.
Family of clientIndividual client plus dependent spouse, dependent children and dependent parents.
Trading callsIntraday, ultra-short, non-delivery or non-personalised recommendations; not investment advice under the IA Regulations.
MITCMost Important Terms and Conditions that must form part of every investment advisory agreement.
CeFCoMCentralised Fee Collection Mechanism run by BSE through which clients may optionally pay IA fees.
BASLBSE Administration and Supervision Limited, the body recognised to administer and supervise IAs.
Annuity dueA series of equal payments made at the beginning of each period; worth more than an ordinary annuity.
PerpetuityA cash flow that continues forever; valued at C/r.
Leverage ratioTotal liabilities divided by total assets.
Liquidity ratioLiquid assets divided by monthly expenses; shows months of cover.
Avalanche methodPaying off the highest-interest debt first.
HypothecationA charge on a movable asset that stays with the borrower, e.g. a car loan.
NovationClearing corporation stepping in as counterparty to both sides of every trade.
Credit spreadExtra yield of a corporate bond over a G-Sec of the same maturity.
Macaulay durationWeighted average time to receive a bond's cash flows in PV terms; equals maturity only for a zero-coupon bond.
STRIPSZero-coupon securities created by separating a G-Sec's coupons and principal.
Cost of carryInterest and holding cost that explains why futures trade above spot.
Open interestNumber of outstanding derivative contracts not yet closed.
High-water markPrevious peak portfolio value above which a PMS can again charge performance fees.
Hurdle rateMinimum return that must be exceeded before profit-sharing fees apply.
Accredited investorInvestor meeting SEBI income/net-worth tests, eligible for lower AIF tickets and other flexibilities.
Efficient frontierSet of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk.
Investment Policy StatementRoad map listing a client's objectives, constraints, risk tolerance and review process.
Tactical asset allocationShort-term deviations from the strategic mix to exploit market opportunities.
TWRRTime-weighted rate of return: chain-linked sub-period returns that neutralise client cash-flow timing.
Tracking errorStandard deviation of the difference between portfolio and benchmark returns.
Jensen alphaReturn earned above the CAPM-required return.
TransmissionTransfer of investments to joint holders, nominees or heirs on an investor's death.
SCORESSEBI's online complaint redress system.
Smart ODROnline dispute resolution portal for securities-market disputes after internal and SCORES routes.
