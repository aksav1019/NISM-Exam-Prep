Investment Adviser – Level 2

From knowing products to advising whole lives: insurance, retirement, tax, estate, behaviour, and Excel-heavy cases.

Level 1 taught you what the financial products are. Level 2 teaches you how to stitch them into one plan for a real family: how much life cover they need, how big the retirement pot must be, what tax each product attracts, who inherits what if someone dies without a Will, and why clients panic-sell. Think of a family doctor: Level 1 is the medicines, and Level 2 is diagnosing the whole patient and writing the prescription. SEBI wants every Registered Investment Adviser (RIA) to pass both levels so that advice is suitable and in the client's interest, not product-pushing.

  • In each case, read all 5 sub-questions first, set the zero date, decide type 0 or 1, and build one Excel table you can reuse.
  • Negative marking: −0.25 per wrong MCQ and −0.5 per wrong case answer. Answer when you can eliminate two options, and skip pure guesses.

  • For estate questions, list the surviving heirs, keep only the highest class present, and count shares.

Basics of Insurance

Insurance moves risk from you (the insured) to a pooled fund (the insurer). The chapter explains the two foundation principles (utmost good faith, insurable interest), the claim-mechanics vocabulary (indemnity, defined benefit, subrogation, contribution, co-pay, deductible), the insurance-planning process, and the key IRDAI/SEBI rules.

Make it stickThe workbook's merchant ships: 100 merchants each risk a ₹10 lakh ship, and about 2 sink every month. If all chip in ₹20,000, the ₹20 lakh pool pays for the 2 losses. If only 40 join, each must pay ₹50,000. That is why a large pool of exposure units makes insurance cheap.
  1. Utmost good faith (Uberrimae Fidei): the insured must disclose every material fact, and if in doubt, disclose. Suppression lets the insurer reject the claim entirely.
  2. Insurable interest: you must be worse off if the risk happens. Spouse, children and parents have it. Aunts, uncles, cousins, nieces and nephews have it only with proof of financial dependence. A lender has it up to the outstanding loan, and an employer in a key employee.
  3. Indemnity policies (health/mediclaim, property, car) pay the actual ascertained loss. Defined-benefit policies (life, critical illness, daily hospital cash) pay a fixed sum.
  4. Subrogation: after paying, the insurer steps into your shoes to recover from the at-fault party. A policy without subrogation would cost more.
  5. Contribution applies only to indemnity policies: each insurer pays its proportionate share. In health insurance the insured chooses the insurer, and contribution applies only if the claim exceeds that policy's sum insured.
  6. Co-pay is a % of the claim borne by the insured. A deductible is a fixed first slice borne by the insured. Apply the deductible first, then the co-pay. Car insurance has a compulsory ₹1,000 deductible up to 1500 cc.
  7. IRDAI 2024 rules: cashless authorisation within 1 hour and final discharge authorisation within 3 hours. 'Cashless Everywhere' extends cashless treatment to non-network hospitals.
  8. Beneficial nominee (Insurance Act s.39(7), 2015 amendment): if the nominee is a parent, spouse or child, they own the money and do not hold it as trustee for the legal heirs.
  9. Section 45 (incontestability): no life policy can be called in question on any ground after 3 years from the policy date, risk commencement or revival, whichever is later. Within 3 years it can be repudiated for fraud.
  10. Health moratorium: after 5 continuous years of premiums, no claim is contestable except for proven fraud or permanent exclusions.
  11. ULIPs: benefit illustrations at 4% and 8%, 5-year lock-in, no surrender charge after 5 years, limited-pay term of at least 5 years, revival within 3 years.
  12. Health portability is available for any policy except group, top-up and super top-up. Group cover can be ported to an individual policy with the same insurer in the first year, and to another insurer from the second year.
  13. Intermediaries: a composite insurance agent can represent at most one life, one general, one health and one of each mono-line insurer. A composite corporate agent can tie up with up to 3 life, 3 general and 3 health insurers. A bank acting as corporate agent is bancassurance. A direct broker represents the client and can charge fees.
  14. Individual health and life insurance policies are exempt from GST from 22 September 2025. An insurance agent or broker advising only on insurance products is exempt from the SEBI IA Regulations.

Life Insurance Products

How much life cover a family needs (Human Life Value, needs-based and combination methods), what each product type really delivers (term, return-of-premium, endowment, whole life, ULIP, mortgage cover), policy facilities such as loans, assignment and nomination, and why MWPA policies matter.

Make it stickAnil earns ₹1.5 lakh a month. After his own expenses, EMI and premiums, ₹83,000 a month reaches his family. Discounting that at the inflation-adjusted rate of 1.89% for his wife's 31 remaining years gives a ₹2.36 crore corpus. Adding the ₹40 lakh loan and deducting existing cover and investments shows he needs about ₹1.26 crore more cover.
  1. Minimum sum assured after the 2024 IRDAI changes: 7× annual premium if below age 50, 5× if above 50.
  2. HLV (income replacement) = PV of future income available to dependants, discounted at the real rate [(1+return)/(1+income growth)] − 1. Higher income growth gives a higher HLV, and HLV falls with age.
  3. If income growth equals the investment return, the real rate is 0 and HLV = current income × years to retirement.
  4. Needs-based method: PV of the family's expense needs, plus liabilities, minus existing investments (excluding the home and personal jewellery), minus existing cover. HLV is always ≥ needs-based because expenditure cannot exceed income.
  5. Term insurance is pure risk cover and the cheapest way to cover the full need. With return of premium, the extra premium earns a modest implied return (the workbook example gives 4.88% via Excel RATE).
  6. Endowment and whole-life policies typically give an inherent return of about 3–5%, and it is not guaranteed. To evaluate, decouple the insurance part from the investment part and check that the cover is adequate first.
  7. Guaranteed surrender value (traditional, after 2 years' full premiums): 30% of premiums paid in year 2, 35% in year 3, 50% in years 4–7, rising to 90% in the last two years. Single premium: 75% in the first 3 years, rising to 90%.
  8. ULIP discontinuance charge is capped at ₹6,000. The fund goes to a discontinued policy fund and is paid after the 5-year lock-in.
  9. Paid-up value = Sum assured × (premiums paid ÷ premiums due).
  10. Assignment cancels the nomination, except an assignment to the insurer for a policy loan, where the nomination is affected only to the insurer's interest. The nomination revives on re-assignment.
  11. MWPA policy: nominees can only be the wife and/or children (not parents). It cannot be changed, survives divorce, is outside the husband's creditors' reach, and the declaration must be made at purchase.
  12. ULIP maturity is tax-free only if sum assured ≥ 10× premium AND (for policies on or after 1 Feb 2021) annual premium ≤ ₹2.5 lakh. Otherwise it is taxed as capital gains. Traditional policies on or after 1 Apr 2023 with premium > ₹5 lakh are taxed as Income from Other Sources.
  13. Riders: critical illness pays on diagnosis plus 30 days' survival (an accelerated version reduces the death cover), waiver of premium, guaranteed insurability, income benefit, and accident/disability.
  14. Telescopic premiums are cheaper per thousand on larger covers, so split large covers (e.g. 4 × ₹2 crore) to drop policies later. Foreign policies can be bought under LRS (USD 2,50,000), but foreign law governs grievances.

Non-Life Insurance Products

General insurance protects assets and income from big unexpected bills: health, motor, property, personal accident, critical illness, travel, liability, fidelity, D&O and keyman. The star topic is how base, top-up and super top-up health plans interact.

Make it stickBase cover of ₹3 lakh, and three bills of ₹2, 3 and 4 lakh in a year. With a top-up (₹5 lakh threshold per claim), you pay ₹6 lakh yourself, because no single bill crosses ₹5 lakh. With a super top-up (₹3 lakh aggregate threshold), you pay nothing, because the base policy's ₹3 lakh already used up the threshold.
  1. Non-life policies typically run 1 year (health and two-wheeler can be 2–3 years). Premium depends on the sum insured and the assessed risk.
  2. Restore benefit refills the sum insured to 100% on partial or full exhaustion. Recharge refills it only when it is reduced by a claim.
  3. Motor: third-party liability cover is compulsory, own-damage cover is not. The no-claim bonus can reach 50% after 5 claim-free years, belongs to the owner (not the car), and is transferable to a new car or a new insurer.
  4. Health: in-patient means at least 24 hours or overnight admission. Day-care and domiciliary treatment can be covered. Pre-existing diseases are excluded for a waiting period. Portability carries over no-claim bonus and time-bound exclusion credit only if there was no break.
  5. Personal accident covers permanent total, permanent partial and temporary total disablement, and the need should be calculated like life cover.
  6. Critical illness pays a lump sum on diagnosis (minimum survival of about 30 days, with the table saying 15–30 days) with no death payment. It is an income-replacement tool, usually standalone or an accelerated rider.
  7. Liability cover pays third-party damages and defence costs (e.g. professional indemnity for advisers) but never intentional or criminal acts. Fidelity cover protects an employer against employee fraud. D&O covers directors' and officers' personal liability.
  8. Keyman insurance is life cover bought and owned by the company on a key executive, with the company as beneficiary.
  9. Multiple indemnity policies make little sense because the total claim cannot exceed the loss. Multiple defined-benefit policies all pay.
  10. Top-up applies the threshold to EACH claim. Super top-up applies the threshold to the AGGREGATE of claims in the year.
  11. Keep base and top-up with the same insurer if possible. With a super top-up, inform that insurer even of claims paid by the base policy so it can track the threshold.
  12. Health (indemnity) normally covers India only. Life, CI and accident (defined benefit) pay wherever the event happens. Overseas travel policies are indemnity products designed country by country.

Retirement Planning Basics

Retirement is the goal with the longest accumulation and distribution periods and the biggest corpus, and no bank will lend you money for it. The chapter covers the planning steps, the effect of inflation and returns, the replacement-ratio and expense-protection methods, and how employer superannuation funds work.

Make it stickAt 6% inflation, something costing ₹100 today costs ₹179 in 10 years, ₹321 in 20 and ₹574 in 30. A retiree who needs ₹60,000 a month at 60 will need ₹1,07,451 at 70 and ₹1,92,428 at 80 for the same lifestyle.
  1. Retirement phases: preparation, pre-retirement (initial), and final retirement.
  2. Unlike education or a house, retirement cannot be funded by a loan, and it is often the goal that gets sacrificed for others.
  3. Planning steps: determine expenses in retirement, then income required, then time horizon (years to retirement and years in retirement), then the corpus.
  4. Corpus variables: periodic income needed, inflation, return on the corpus, and the retirement period. Inflation pushes the corpus up and return pulls it down.
  5. Real (inflation-adjusted) return = [(1 + return) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1.
  6. Longevity risk means underestimating years in retirement. Health and travel costs usually inflate faster than general inflation.
  7. Replacement ratio method: target a % (e.g. 80%) of pre-retirement income, then grow it by inflation each year.
  8. Expense protection method: build the retirement budget from actual expenses (some rise, like medical and travel; some fall, like EMIs and children). It is easier for people near retirement.
  9. An employer cannot pay pensions out of current revenue (AS-15 requires accrual, and s.2(11) of the Insurance Act puts annuities with life insurers). The options are an insurer (LIC or another IRDAI-approved insurer) or an irrevocable superannuation trust.
  10. Approved superannuation fund (Part B, Schedule XI, IT Act 2025): irrevocable trust, at least 90% of employees employed in India, sole purpose is annuities, the employer must contribute, and benefits are payable only in India.
  11. Superannuation tax: employer contributions are deductible for the employer, fund income is exempt, specified payments are exempt, and the employee's own contribution is deductible only under the old regime.
  12. Statutory pension now comes under the Employees' Pension Scheme, 2026 (Code on Social Security, 2020), which supersedes EPS 1995.

Retirement Products

This chapter has the most numbers in the book. Accumulation products (EPF, VPF, PPF, gratuity, superannuation, NPS with its new drawdown/RIS/UPS/Vatsalya rules, APY, SEBI life-cycle funds) and distribution products (annuities, SWP, bond/FD ladders, SCSS, PMVVY, POMIS, reverse mortgage).

Make it stickThink of the retirement toolkit as a two-room house. The 'accumulation room' (EPF, PPF, NPS) fills the tank while you work, and the 'distribution room' (annuity, SCSS, SWP, ladders, reverse mortgage) is the tap that releases it after you stop.
  1. EPF: 12% of basic + DA from employee and employer. The employer's 12% splits into EPF 3.67%, EPS 8.33% (capped on a ₹25,000 salary per the workbook), EDLI 0.5% and admin 0.5%. The rate is 10% for firms with fewer than 20 employees. New women employees contribute 8% for 3 years while the employer still pays 12%.
  2. EPF interest is set by the CBT with the Ministry of Finance. Interest on employee contributions (EPF + VPF) above ₹2.5 lakh a year is taxable. An account is inactive after 3 years without transactions and inoperative at age 58. After 7 years inoperative the money moves to the Senior Citizens' Welfare Fund (claimable within 25 years). VPF: up to 100% of basic + DA with no employer match, and the same tax treatment as EPF.
  3. EPF withdrawal: 75% after 1 month of unemployment, the rest after 2 months. Partial withdrawals: marriage or education 50% of the employee share after 7 years, house 36 months' wages after 5 years, home-loan repayment after 10 years, renovation after 5 years, 90% at age 54. Withdrawal after 5 continuous years is tax-free. Before 5 years, TDS is 10% if the amount exceeds ₹50,000 and PAN is given.
  4. PPF: residents only (NRIs earn no interest after 30 Sep 2024). ₹500–₹1.5 lakh a year, 15-year lock-in, extendable in 5-year blocks (with contribution needs Form-4 within 1 year of maturity, with a 60% withdrawal cap). Loan from FY 3 to FY 6 of up to 25% of the balance at the end of the 2nd preceding year. Partial withdrawal after 5 FYs, once a year, of the lower of 50% of the balance at the end of the preceding year or the 4th preceding year. EEE under the old regime.
  5. Gratuity (Code on Social Security, 2020): 5 years' continuous service (waived on death or disablement), establishments with 10+ employees. Formula = 15 × last monthly wages × years ÷ 26, with a part-year over 6 months rounded up. Tax exemption cap is ₹20 lakh. Central government cap is ₹25 lakh.
  6. Superannuation commutation: up to 1/3 of the annuity if the employee also gets gratuity, 1/2 if not. Aggregate employer contribution to EPF + NPS + superannuation above ₹7.5 lakh is a taxable perquisite.
  7. NPS: All-Citizen model for ages 18–85. Active choice allows up to 75% equity. Life-cycle funds are LC25, LC50, LC75 and Aggressive (50% equity up to 45, 35% at 55+). The Multiple Scheme Framework (from 1 Oct 2025) for non-government subscribers allows up to 100% equity with a 15-year vesting period. Pension fund manager can be changed once a year, and allocation four times a year.
  8. NPS partial withdrawal: after 3 years, 25% of own contribution, up to 4 times before 60 with a 4-year gap (a 3-year gap after 60).
  9. NPS exit at 60 (non-government): at least 20% annuity (government subscribers 40%). Corpus ≤ ₹8 lakh can be taken in full. ₹8–12 lakh: up to ₹6 lakh lump sum, the rest as annuity or SUR for at least 6 years. Premature exit: at least 80% annuity, but ≤ ₹5 lakh can be taken in full. Joining after 60: 20% annuity, ≤ ₹12 lakh in full. Can stay invested and defer up to age 85.
  10. NPS tax: lump sum exempt up to 60% of the corpus, partial withdrawal exempt up to 25% of own contribution, annuity income taxable. Deductions: 80CCD(1) up to 10% of salary or 20% of GTI, plus ₹50,000 under 1B (old regime only). Employer contribution deduction is 14% (government, and private employers under the new regime) or 10% (private employers under the old regime).
  11. Drawdown/RIS (PFRDA, 15 May 2026): RIS Steady glides equity from 35% at 60 to 10% at 75. SPR = 1 ÷ (end age − current age), reset each birthday. SUR = equal units over the period. The pension fund can be switched once every two FYs. Mandatory annuitisation is unaffected.
  12. UPS (from 1 Apr 2025, central government): employee 10% + matching employer 10% + 8.5% government pool. Payout = 50% of the 12-month average basic after 25 years. Minimum ₹10,000 a month after 10 years. Family payout 60%. Lump sum of 1/10 of basic + DA per completed 6 months. One-way switch from UPS to NPS allowed up to 1 year before superannuation.
  13. NPS Vatsalya (minors): minimum ₹250 a year. Partial withdrawal after 3 years of up to 25%, twice before 18 and twice between 18 and 21. Deduction up to ₹50,000 under s.124(4) (old regime). APY: ages 18–40, pension ₹1,000–5,000 guaranteed by the GoI, not open to income-tax payers from 1 Oct 2022.
  14. Distribution products: life annuity without return of purchase price pays the HIGHEST annuity, with return of purchase price the LOWEST. SCSS: 60+, 5 years extendable in 3-year blocks, maximum ₹30 lakh, quarterly interest that is fully taxable. POMIS: ₹9 lakh single / ₹15 lakh joint, 5 years, 2% penalty if closed in years 1–3 and 1% in years 3–5. Reverse mortgage: 60+ (spouse 55+), house with 20+ years' residual life, maximum 20-year tenure, ₹50,000 a month cap, receipts tax-exempt.

Miscellaneous Aspects of Retirement Planning

The adviser's role, Excel corpus maths (PV, PMT), and the big 2026 addition: why deterministic calculations can mislead and how the stochastic Safe Withdrawal Rate (SWR) and sequence-of-returns risk change corpus sizing. It also covers step-up savings, the cost of early EPF withdrawals, and starting a philanthropy conversation.

Make it stickRetirees A and B both average 0% over 5 years and withdraw ₹6 a year from ₹100. A suffers −20% and −10% first and ends with ₹59. B gets +20% and +10% first and ends with ₹71. Same average, different order, different outcome. That is sequence-of-returns risk.
  1. Retirement expenses are roughly 50–60% of current expenses if there are no liabilities, but medical costs rise.
  2. Corpus (deterministic) = PV(real rate, years in retirement, −annual expense at retirement, , type). Monthly saving = PMT(rate/12, months, , corpus). Delaying the start sharply raises the saving required (₹3,376 at 30 vs ₹11,928 at 40 for a ₹1.18 crore goal at 12%).
  3. Deterministic methods assume fixed returns and inflation every year, so they ignore volatility and sequence risk. A 1994 back-test of a deterministic 5.55% withdrawal ran out in 2016, about 7 years and 5 months early, even though the averages were correct.
  4. Stochastic (Monte Carlo) methods test thousands of return paths and measure failure probability.
  5. SWR = the maximum FIRST-YEAR withdrawal, as a % of the starting corpus, that then rises with inflation and survives with at least 95% probability (failure ≤ 5%).
  6. After year 1, the rupee withdrawal grows by inflation regardless of the portfolio value. It is NOT re-computed as a % of the current corpus.
  7. Bengen's 4% rule (1994) uses US data. For India, research suggests about 3–3.5% for a 30-year horizon.
  8. SWR table (India): 10–30% equity gives the highest SWRs. Examples: 30% equity over 30 years = 3.70%, 40% equity over 30 years = 3.60%, 40% over 40 years = 2.95%, 100% over 30 years = 2.56%. A longer horizon means a lower SWR.
  9. Corpus via SWR = annual income needed at retirement ÷ SWR.
  10. Step-up: as income rises 10%, step up the SIP by at least 5–7%. Early EPF withdrawals destroy compounding (₹75,000 withdrawn 30 years early is about ₹8.66 lakh lost at 8.5%). Transfer EPF via the 12-digit UAN. NPS has one PRAN for life.
  11. Product evaluation by stage: pre-retirement (15–20 years away) looks at cost, return, risk and tax efficiency. In retirement, income products are judged on inflation-beating and capital protection, with a growth bucket of 10–15 years. Post-retirement (75+): capital protection, low risk, liquidity for heirs.
  12. Philanthropy: raise it at the right time (a liquidity event, Will drafting, life events, the annual review) and follow up.

Concepts in Taxation

The Income-tax Act, 2025 now applies from FY 2026-27 with a single 'Tax Year'. The chapter covers residential status, the five heads of income, clubbing, set-off and carry-forward of losses, Chapter VIII deductions, the s.156 (87A) rebate, DTAA, EEE/EET/ETE and the maximum marginal rate.

Make it stickMr. C returns from London and spends 298 days in India this year, but only 578 days in the previous 7 years. He is resident because of the 182-day test, yet Not Ordinarily Resident because he stayed ≤729 days in 7 years. So his London interest stays out of Indian tax for now.
  1. The IT Act 2025 and IT Rules 2026 apply from FY 2026-27. 'Tax Year' (e.g. 1 Apr 2026 – 31 Mar 2027) replaces previous year and assessment year. Old circulars apply to the corresponding new provisions.
  2. Resident (Indian citizen): 182+ days, OR 60+ days in the year plus 365+ days in the 4 preceding years. For a citizen visiting with Indian income above ₹15 lakh, the 60-day test becomes 120 days. Crew members and those leaving for jobs need 182 days.
  3. Deemed resident: an Indian citizen with Indian income above ₹15 lakh who is not taxable elsewhere. Always treated as RNOR.
  4. NOR if non-resident in 9 of the 10 preceding years OR ≤729 days in the 7 preceding years. An ROR is taxed on worldwide income. An RNOR is taxed on Indian income plus foreign income from a business controlled or profession set up in India. An NR is taxed on Indian income only.
  5. An HUF is resident unless its control and management are wholly outside India.
  6. Five heads: Salary, House Property, Business/Profession, Capital Gains, Other Sources. Standard deduction is ₹50,000 (old) / ₹75,000 (new).
  7. Employer NPS contribution is added to salary and then deducted (s.124(1)) up to 14%, available even under the new regime. EPF employer contribution is not added to salary. All employer EPF + NPS + superannuation above ₹7.5 lakh is a perquisite. The '12% EPF' test was deleted from Tax Year 2026-27.
  8. House property: up to two self-occupied houses, a 30% standard deduction on NAV, self-occupied interest up to ₹2 lakh (old regime only, ₹30,000 if construction takes more than 5 years), and pre-construction interest in 5 equal instalments. HP loss set-off against other heads is capped at ₹2 lakh (old regime only, none under the new regime). Carry forward 8 years against HP income only.
  9. Capital losses: LTCL only against LTCG. STCL against both STCG and LTCG. Never against other heads. Carry forward 8 years if the return is filed on time.
  10. Business loss: set off against any head except salary, carried 8 years. Speculative loss only against speculative profit, carried 4 years. Unabsorbed depreciation carries forward indefinitely. Loss under Other Sources cannot be carried forward.
  11. Deductions (old regime): s.123 (80C) ₹1.5 lakh, 80CCD(1B) ₹50,000, 80D ₹75,000 / ₹1,00,000, 80TTA ₹10,000, 80TTB ₹50,000 for seniors, 80E no limit. Employer NPS is available in both regimes.
  12. Rebate s.156 (87A): old regime up to ₹12,500 if income ≤ ₹5 lakh. New regime up to ₹60,000 if income ≤ ₹12 lakh, with marginal relief (effectively up to ₹12,71,250). Not available against s.198 (112A) LTCG tax.

  14. EEE = PPF. EET = ELSS (as given in the workbook). ETE = 5-year tax-saver FD, SCSS. MMR = 30% + 37% surcharge + 4% cess = 42.744%.

What counts as a capital asset, short-term vs long-term holding periods, what is and is not a 'transfer', how to compute gains (cost, grandfathering, the indexation parallel test for old property), tax rates, and the reinvestment exemptions (ss.82–88).

Make it stickFlat bought in FY 2012-13 for ₹50 lakh and sold in FY 2025-26 for ₹1.2 crore. The new method taxes ₹70 lakh at 12.5% = ₹8.75 lakh. The old method indexes the cost to ₹94 lakh (×376/200) and taxes ₹26 lakh at 20% = ₹5.2 lakh. Because it was bought before 23 July 2024, the owner pays the lower figure, ₹5.2 lakh.

  2. General LTCG threshold is more than 24 months. 12 months for listed shares and listed securities, units of equity-oriented funds (listed or unlisted), UTI units, zero-coupon bonds and taxable ULIPs.

Income from Other Sources

The residual head: dividends, interest on securities, lottery or online-game winnings, gifts and deemed income. The 2026 changes are that no expenses can be deducted from dividends and that buyback gains are now capital gains.

Make it stickPriya receives ₹40,000 of shares from a college friend and ₹30,000 from her mother's sister (mausi) in the same year. The friend's gift is tested against the ₹50,000 limit. The mausi is a 'relative', so her gift is fully excluded. Total from non-relatives is ₹40,000, under the limit, so nothing is taxable.
  1. Always under Other Sources: dividends, interest on securities held as investment, forfeited advance on a failed asset sale, deemed income on gifts or under-priced receipts, and specified sums from business trusts.
  2. Since DDT was abolished (from AY 2021-22), dividends are taxed in the shareholder's hands at slab rates. From Tax Year 2026-27, NO expense (not even interest) can be deducted from dividend income. The earlier 20% interest allowance is gone.
  3. Buyback: from 1 Oct 2024 it was taxed as dividend. From Tax Year 2026-27 it is taxed as CAPITAL GAINS (consideration − cost, STCG or LTCG by holding period). Promoters pay an additional tax.
  4. Interest on securities is taxed per the regular accounting method (mercantile = accrual, cash = receipt). Commission paid to realise the interest is deductible.
  5. Gift rule: money or property received without (or for inadequate) consideration is taxable if the aggregate in the year exceeds ₹50,000, in which case the WHOLE amount is taxed, not just the excess.
  6. Exempt occasions: marriage of the individual, under a Will or inheritance, in contemplation of death, from a 'relative', from a local authority, and from specified charitable or educational institutions.
  7. Relatives include spouse, children, parents, siblings and their spouses, the spouse's siblings, lineal ascendants and descendants, and parents' siblings (chacha, bua, mama, mausi) with their spouses.
  8. NOT relatives: nephew, niece, cousins, step-brother/sister.
  9. Foreign-currency interest is converted at the SBI TT buying rate on the last day of the month before the due date.

Taxation of Debt Products

How interest and capital gains on bonds, G-secs, T-bills, tax-free bonds, debt mutual funds and market-linked debentures are taxed, plus non-resident rules (DTAA, Chapter XII-A concessions, foreign retirement accounts).

Make it stickXYZ Bank held unlisted G-secs for 12 years and still got short-term capital gains at normal rates, because unlisted bonds and debentures are always short-term now. Holding them longer changes nothing.
  1. Interest is taxed under Other Sources at slab rates (accrual or receipt per the method of accounting). On sale, accrued but unpaid interest is separated from the sale price as interest.
  2. Listed bonds and debentures: LTCG after 12 months at 12.5% without indexation. Unlisted bonds and debentures are always short-term at slab.
  3. T-bills (91, 182, 364 days) and Cash Management Bills (under 91 days) are issued at a discount. The gain is short-term at slab.
  4. Dated G-secs run 5–40 years with half-yearly coupons. States issue SDLs. No TDS on G-sec interest to residents, except the 8% Savings (Taxable) Bonds 2003 and 7.75% Savings (Taxable) Bonds 2018 when interest exceeds ₹10,000.
  5. Tax-free bonds: interest is exempt, but capital gains on sale or redemption are taxable like any bond.
  6. Mutual funds for tax: equity-oriented, Specified MF (≥65% debt/money market, bought on or after 1 Apr 2023: always STCG at slab, no cost of improvement), and other MFs (LTCG 12.5% after 12 months if listed or 24 months if unlisted).
  7. MLDs (issuers need net worth ≥ ₹100 crore, typical tenure 12–36 months): gains are always short-term at slab under s.76, even for MLDs bought before 2023 if redeemed after 1 Apr 2023.
  8. Non-residents: dividends taxed at 20% (subject to DTAA). The more beneficial of the Act and the DTAA applies. A TRC plus Form 41 (old Form 10F) is required.
  9. Chapter XII-A (ss.212–218) for NRIs and PIOs on assets bought in foreign exchange: investment income at 20%, LTCG at 12.5%, LTCG exempt if reinvested within 6 months (taxed back if the new asset is sold within 3 years). Can continue after becoming resident for debt income.

Taxation of Equity Products

Transaction costs, dividends, listed and unlisted shares, preference shares and warrants, equity mutual funds, F&O (business income), bonus stripping and the exemption-limit adjustment.

Make it stickRavi buys 1,000 shares at ₹106 within 3 months of the record date, gets a 1:1 bonus, and sells the originals at ₹95 within 9 months. The ₹11,000 loss is ignored and becomes the cost of the bonus shares, ₹11 each. Bonus stripping is neutralised.
  1. Costs: brokerage, STT (paid to the government by the 7th of the next month), unified stamp duty, exchange charges, SEBI turnover fee of ₹10 per crore, DP charges, and 18% GST on brokerage and charges.
  2. Dividends from foreign companies are taxed at 5–15% under most DTAAs. A non-resident pays 20% on Indian dividends (10% from an IFSC unit).
  3. FIFO applies account-wise for demat holdings, and for dematerialised physical shares the date of entry into the account counts.
  4. s.198 (112A) needs STT on both purchase and sale (with CBDT exceptions such as pre-1 Oct 2004 shares, IPO and preferential allotments, ESOPs, and IFSC deals in foreign currency). LTCG above ₹1.25 lakh is taxed at 12.5%.

  6. Unlisted shares: LTCG after 24 months at 12.5%, STCG at slab. Shares sold in an IPO's offer for sale fall under s.198.
  7. Surcharge on 111A/112/112A capital gains and on dividends is capped at 15%.
  8. Equity-oriented fund: at least 65% in listed domestic equity (a fund of funds needs 90% in such a fund, which itself must hold 90%), measured on the annual average of monthly averages. ELSS has a 3-year lock-in, the shortest among tax-saving instruments.

  11. Option exercised: the cost of the shares = strike price + premium paid.

Taxation of Other Products

ESOPs, Sovereign Gold Bonds, NPS, REITs and InvITs, AIFs, ETFs, life insurance and reverse mortgage: each with its own tax quirks.

Make it stickMr B exercises 100 ESOPs at ₹500 when the FMV is ₹6,500 and pays salary tax on ₹6 lakh. Later, the capital gain is computed from ₹6,500 (FMV on exercise), and the holding period counts from the allotment date.
  1. ESOP stages: grant, vesting, exercise, allotment. Perquisite = (FMV on EXERCISE date − exercise price) × shares, taxed as salary in the year of allotment. Cost for capital gains = that FMV. Holding period runs from ALLOTMENT.
  2. Eligible start-ups (s.140 = 80-IAC: incorporated 1 Apr 2016 to 1 Apr 2030, turnover ≤ ₹300 crore, IMB certificate) can defer TDS and tax on the ESOP perquisite.

Tax Provisions for Special Cases

Corporate actions and special situations: bonus, split or consolidation, buyback, rights, mergers and demergers, securities lending, preference conversion, segregated MF portfolios, MF consolidation and winding up. The annexures give tax rates and CII.

Make it stickA fund side-pockets a defaulted bond. Your ₹15,000 cost (₹15 × 1,000 units) is divided in the ratio of NAVs: segregated NAV ₹2 against total NAV ₹12 means ₹2,500 goes to the segregated units and ₹12,500 stays with the main portfolio. The holding period for both runs from your original purchase.
  1. Bonus: no tax on allotment. Cost is nil (FMV on 1 Apr 2001 if issued before then). Holding counts from allotment. Grandfathering can apply.
  2. Split or consolidation: not a transfer. The original cost is spread over the new number of shares, and the holding period runs from the original purchase.
  3. Buyback (from Tax Year 2026-27): capital gains for the shareholder, with no tax on the company. Promoter additional tax: STCG 2% (domestic company promoter) or 10% (others), LTCG 9.5% or 17.5%.
  4. Rights renunciation: cost nil, holding from offer to renunciation, taxed as STCG at slab (NOT s.196). A buyer of renounced rights has a cost of the price paid to the renouncer plus the price paid to the company.
  5. Mergers: shares received in amalgamation carry the amalgamating company's cost and holding period. Demerger cost is split in the ratio of the net book value transferred to net worth.
  6. Securities lending (SEBI scheme): not a transfer. The lending fee is business income or Other Sources. Tenure is 1 day to 12 months including rollovers. The lender deposits 25% margin (nil if lending the same day) and the borrower 100% plus margins. Only F&O stocks and liquid index ETFs are eligible.
  7. Preference converted to equity: not a transfer. Cost and holding of the preference shares carry over.
  8. Segregated portfolio cost = original cost × (NAV of segregated assets ÷ total NAV before segregation). The main portfolio cost is reduced by the same amount.
  9. MF consolidation (equity with equity, or non-equity with non-equity, schemes or plans) is not a transfer. Cost and holding carry over.
  10. MF winding up: triggered by trustees, a 75% unit-holder resolution, or a SEBI direction. Notice goes in two daily newspapers plus a vernacular one. A simple majority of unit-holders authorises the winding up. Proceeds are taxed like a redemption.
  11. Old-regime slabs: nil to ₹2.5 lakh (₹3 lakh for seniors, ₹5 lakh for super seniors), 5%, 20%, 30%. Surcharge 10%, 15%, 25% or 37% (new regime capped at 25%). Cess 4%. VDA and online games 30%. Undisclosed income 60%.

Basics of Estate Planning

Estate planning means accumulating, conserving and distributing an estate so the family stays financially secure. The chapter covers what makes up an estate, what happens on intestacy, which succession law applies to whom, the Hindu and Muslim succession rules, MWPA and mutation.

Make it stickA Hindu man dies without a Will, leaving a widow, his mother, two sons, a daughter, his father, and the widow and son of a predeceased son. Class I heirs share: widow 1, mother 1, each son 1, daughter 1, and the predeceased son's branch 1 (split equally between his widow and son). That is 6 shares. The father, a Class II heir, gets nothing.
  1. Estate planning is for everyone, not just the rich: families with minors, high liabilities, property in many states, small businesses, or disability risk. India currently has no estate duty.
  2. Estate planning (personal assets, effective at death) ≠ succession planning (business continuity, done in one's lifetime).
  3. Constituents: gross estate (everything owned, including NPS and insurance), residue estate (personal effects, unallocated assets, sums due), and estate debt (loans, taxes, cases).
  4. Dying intestate (s.30, Indian Succession Act): having no testamentary disposition capable of taking effect. Intestacy can be total or partial.
  5. Laws: for Hindus, Jains, Sikhs and Buddhists, testamentary succession follows the ISA 1925 and intestate succession the Hindu Succession Act 1956 (amended 2005). Christians and Parsis follow the ISA for both. Muslims follow personal law (Shariat Act 1937 for intestacy).
  6. A succession certificate is used for specified debts and securities. It is not universal and does not decide title to immovable property.
  7. Minimum estate plan: a last Will and testament plus a living Will. Other elements are a trust, a power of attorney, and nominations.
  8. Hindu male intestacy: Class I heirs (16, including son, daughter, widow, mother and the branches of predeceased children) take simultaneously. Then Class II (father is entry I, then siblings…). Then agnates (related wholly through males). Then cognates (not wholly through males).
  9. Class I distribution: widows together take 1 share. Each son, daughter and the mother take 1 share. Each predeceased child's branch takes 1 share. Within one Class II entry, heirs share equally.
  10. Uterine siblings (same mother, different father) are excluded. Half blood means same father, different mother. Adopted children count as full blood. Children of void or voidable marriages inherit from their parents.
  11. Hindu female (s.15): first sons, daughters (and the children of predeceased ones) and husband, then the husband's heirs, then mother and father, then the father's heirs, then the mother's heirs. If she has no children, property from her parents goes back to the father's heirs and property from her husband or father-in-law to the husband's heirs.
  12. Muslim law: 12 sharers plus residuaries. A widow gets 1/4 (no children) or 1/8 (with children). A husband gets 1/2 or 1/4. A sole daughter gets 1/2, multiple daughters share 2/3, and with sons a son gets twice a daughter. There is no birth-right. A Will (Wasiyat) is limited to 1/3 of the net estate without heirs' consent and can be oral. Escheat goes to the government if there are no heirs.
  13. MWPA s.6: a policy taken by a husband for his wife and/or children is a trust outside the husband's control and creditors, paid to the Official Trustee unless special trustees are appointed.
  14. Mutation is an administrative or fiscal change in revenue records. It does NOT confer title.

Tools for Estate Planning

The toolkit: Wills (types, validity, registration, revocation, codicils, executors), probate and succession certificates, gifts, joint holding, nomination (the new SEBI and bank limits), family settlements and family-business planning, trusts (types and taxation), guardianship and powers of attorney.

Make it stickA trust is like a locked toolbox. The settlor puts tools (assets) in, the trustee holds the key and uses them under the deed's instructions, and the beneficiaries enjoy what gets built. Unlike a Will, it works from day one, needs no probate and stays private.
  1. A Will (s.2(h), ISA) takes effect only on death and is revocable any time (s.62). Testator = maker, legatee = recipient, executor = administrator (s.2(c)).
  2. Types: conditional, joint (treated as separate Wills), mutual (reciprocal benefits), duplicate (destroying the one in the testator's custody revokes both), holograph (entirely handwritten), concurrent (e.g. one per country), sham, and privileged (a soldier, airman or mariner in action can make one orally before 2 witnesses).
  3. Essentials (s.59): sound mind, not a minor, free consent, knowledge of contents. A deaf, dumb or blind person can make one, and so can an insane person in a lucid interval.
  4. Registration is OPTIONAL (s.18, Registration Act), on plain paper with no stamp duty. It can be deposited sealed with the Registrar (ss.42–46). Exempt from attending the office: the infirm, jail inmates, and pardanashin women.
  5. Revocation: by a later Will or codicil, a written declaration executed like a Will, or by burning or tearing with intent. Marriage does not revoke a Hindu's Will (s.57), and divorce does not revoke a Will. Losing a Will is not revocation. A codicil (s.2(b)) amends a Will and is executed like one.
  6. Probate (s.2(f)) = a copy of the Will certified under the court's seal. It is granted ONLY to an executor (s.222). A minor executor gets it after majority. Section 213 was omitted by the Repealing and Amending Act, 2025, so probate is no longer compulsory for Mumbai, Chennai or Kolkata Wills.
  7. Letters of administration are issued when there is no executor, the executor is incapable or refuses, or the executor dies before completing the work. A succession certificate (District Judge, Part X) covers specified debts and securities only.
  8. Gift (s.126, Transfer of Property Act): voluntary and without consideration, ordinarily irrevocable. A gift of immovable property needs a registered instrument.
  9. Nomination: only individuals can nominate. A nominee is generally a custodian or trustee for the legal heirs, except beneficial nominees under the Insurance Act and EPF. From 1 Sep 2026, SEBI allows up to 3 nominees for demat accounts and MF folios. Banks allow up to 4, simultaneous or successive (lockers are successive only).
  10. Joint tenancy carries survivorship. Tenants-in-common do not (heirs under s.19 of the HSA take as tenants-in-common). PPF cannot be attached by creditors. About 70% of family businesses survive a generation.
  11. Indian Trusts Act 1882 (private trusts). Parties are the author or settlor, the trustee and the beneficiary (s.3), and a trust needs intention, purpose, beneficiary, property and transfer (s.6). An oral trust is possible only for movables.
  12. A revocable trust is taxed in the settlor's hands (s.97). A discretionary trust (indeterminate shares) is taxed at MMR (s.307), with exceptions. A determinate trust is taxed through the trustee as representative assessee (s.304). If a trust has business income, its entire income is taxed at MMR unless it is the only trust created by Will for dependent relatives.
  13. Trust revocation (s.78): a trust by Will can be revoked at the testator's pleasure. Others need all competent beneficiaries' consent or an express power reserved. Income accumulation is limited to the transferor's life or 18 years. DNI = taxable income − capital gains + tax exemptions.
  14. Power of attorney: general, special, or special for registration. Revoked on revocation, insolvency, renunciation, completion, or the death of either party (s.201). An agent with an interest cannot be revoked (s.202). The presumption of due execution (s.84, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023) applies before a notary, court, consul or government representative. Basics of Behavioural Finance

Why real investors are not the rational robots of standard finance: bounded rationality, prospect theory, emotional biases versus cognitive errors, fusion investing, and how biases create anomalies, bubbles and crashes.

Make it stickIsaac Newton made £3,500 in the South Sea Bubble, re-entered, and lost £20,000. His verdict: 'I can calculate the motions of heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people.'
  1. Standard finance assumes investors are rational, risk-averse, self-interested utility maximisers with full information. Behavioural finance puts psychology at the core, with loss aversion as its base.
  2. Nobel laureates: Herbert Simon (1978) for bounded rationality, Daniel Kahneman (2002), and Richard Thaler (2017) for nudges and mental accounting.
  3. Bounded rationality: limited time, information or ability leads people to 'satisfice' (satisfactory plus sufficient) using rules of thumb. It is rational within limits, not irrational.
  4. Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979): outcomes are judged against a reference point. People are risk-averse in gains and risk-seeking in losses, and losses hurt more. Choice has two phases: framing (editing) and evaluation.
  5. Emotional biases (feelings): loss aversion and the disposition effect (Shefrin & Statman 1985, selling winners early and holding losers), stereotyping, overconfidence (worse with self-attribution, confusing brains with a bull market), endowment, status quo, and regret aversion.
  6. Regret from errors of commission (actions taken) hurts more than errors of omission, which pushes people toward the status quo.
  7. Cognitive errors (faulty reasoning, reducible through vigilance): mental accounting (Thaler, 1999, treating money as non-fungible), framing (95% lean vs 5% fat), anchoring (relying on pre-existing information), and choice paralysis.
  8. Fusion investing combines value and growth fundamentals with behavioural momentum. Graham: the market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run. Screen on value, then fundamental strength (Piotroski score 0–9), then momentum. Transaction costs and taxes matter.
  9. Anomalies are departures of returns from CAPM predictions. The disposition effect explains momentum. Overconfidence and self-attribution drive excessive trading.
  10. Bubbles (Kindleberger 1978) come from liquidity, celebrity status, momentum or herding, and the illusion of control. Redhead (2008) says they are likelier with many inexperienced traders, high value uncertainty, lottery-like payoffs, easy borrowing, and difficult short selling.

Behavioural Finance in Practice

How the adviser spots and manages behaviour: retail therapy, over-trading, chasing past performance, home bias, buying insurance for tax, over- or under-diversification, framing of risk questions and overconfidence. Plus nudges and the adviser's calming role through greed-fear cycles.

Make it stickPaul Samuelson: 'Investing should be dull… like watching paint dry or grass grow. If you want excitement, take $800 and go to Las Vegas.'
  1. Retail therapy: first make the client aware that stress drives it, then give it a fixed budget (even a separate account). Credit cards encourage impulsive spending.
  2. Action bias leads to too many transactions. Prime clients in advance on the value of patience.
  3. Chasing past performance is like driving by the rear-view mirror. Use a pre-set asset allocation with upper limits per asset class and per security.
  4. Home-country bias denies diversification. LRS (USD 2,50,000) and Indian international funds help. The cure is for the adviser to learn about global investing and educate the client.
  5. Buying insurance only to save tax leads to wrong or inadequate cover. Term insurance plus PPF typically beats an endowment for the same outlay.
  6. Diversification comes from asset classes, not from owning many funds. A top-500 index fund can replace 12 overlapping equity funds.
  7. Framing: some clients respond to 'what you gain', others to 'what you lose'. Diagnose the preferred frame and confirm it in discussion.
  8. Overconfidence: after one 5× winner the client wants to overweight. The adviser should stand between the client and the mistake.
  9. Nudges: pre-agreed ground rules, such as rebalancing triggers, automatically book profits high and buy low.
  10. In greed and fear cycles the adviser keeps the client on the planned path, and helps prioritise conflicting goals (e.g. an education loan rather than sacrificing retirement).

Risk Profiling for Investors

Measuring willingness, ability and need to take risk, the SEBI IA Regulation 16 requirements, the behavioural drivers (risk perception, overconfidence, financial engagement, loss aversion, composure), and turning a risk profile into strategic, tactical or dynamic asset allocation and model portfolios.

Make it stickPay ₹1 lakh to call a coin toss and win ₹2.1 lakh if right. The expected gain is +₹5,000, yet most people refuse until the prize rises to ₹2.5–3 lakh. Willingness to take risk is psychology, not maths.
  1. Risk appetite rises with more earning members, a longer life expectancy, youth, employability, a steady job, market knowledge, a daring psyche, a larger capital base and regular income. It falls with more dependants.
  2. Actual risk-taking depends on resources (ability) and how important the payoff is (need).
  3. Risk tolerance is measured with questionnaires and scored into conservative, moderate or aggressive. People who 'guess the right answer' defeat the tool. Transaction history can supplement profiling but never replace it.
  4. Regulation 16, SEBI IA Regulations 2013: obtain client information, assess willingness and ability (including the ability to absorb losses) objectively, use tools fit for purpose, ask fair, clear and non-leading questions, communicate the profile and obtain consent BEFORE advice (including free trials), and update periodically and on material changes.
  5. Behavioural drivers: risk perception (best gauged by asking a direct question), overconfidence (inflates stated tolerance), and financial engagement (knowledge plus involvement leads to better diversification).
  6. Risk tolerance = willingness, e.g. choosing a 50% chance of ₹20,000 over a certain ₹10,000. Loss aversion: a loss hurts about 2× an equal gain, and it also drives the status quo and sunk-cost fallacy. Risk composure = whether stated preferences hold under stress, best assessed from past behaviour in actual falls.
  7. Risk capacity is the objective financial and temporal ability to absorb losses (a 35-year-old has more than a 60-year-old). Risk need is the return required to reach a goal.
  8. If risk need exceeds capacity or tolerance, flag the goal and revise the amount, horizon or savings, rather than simply adding risk.
  9. High tolerance with low composure points to a less aggressive allocation.
  10. Asset allocation explains performance more than security selection. Strategic allocation is long-term and goal-based with periodic rebalancing. Tactical allocation is a market call, only for seasoned investors with large surpluses. Dynamic allocation is model or trigger based and removes subjectivity.
  11. Model portfolios exist for each distinct client profile and are then tweaked (e.g. a couple in their 70s: 15% equity index, 5% gold ETF, 35% debt hybrid/MIS/SCSS, 30% debt/FD, 15% liquid).
  12. Investment objectives: growth (equity, real estate, gold), income (deposits, debt), liquidity (cash, bank deposits, short-term funds), capital preservation (cash, deposits, ultra-short funds). Review at least once a year, and rebalance to the client's needs, not to short-term price moves.

Comparison of Products across Categories

Side-by-side comparisons on risk, return and liquidity: ELSS vs NPS vs PPF/tax-saver FD, MF vs PMS vs AIF Cat III, MF vs ULIP, active vs index, direct equity vs funds, ETF vs index fund, the four routes to gold, real estate vs REIT vs InvIT, debt options, derivatives vs index funds, company deposits vs debentures, and insurance pairs.

Make it stickGold four ways. Physical gold carries GST plus purity and storage worries. Gold funds and gold ETFs remove purity and storage risk but GST is embedded. An SGB has no GST, pays 2.5% interest, and redemption is tax-free if held from issue to maturity.
  1. All products are compared on Risk, Return and Liquidity.
  2. ELSS invests at least 80% in equity with a 3-year lock-in and LTCG at 12.5%. NPS Tier I allows up to 75% equity, alternatives up to 5% for some subscribers, and exit rules apply. PPF carries sovereign risk with a 15-year term. A tax-saver FD has a 5-year lock-in and taxable interest. DICGC insures up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank.
  3. Mutual fund: pooled, minimum around ₹1,000, SEBI-capped fees. PMS: an individual demat account, minimum ₹50 lakh, no upfront fees. AIF Cat III: complex or leveraged strategies, minimum ₹1 crore, no uniform fee cap.
  4. MF vs ULIP: SEBI vs IRDAI caps. ULIP mortality charges are deducted from the fund value, which makes comparison harder. ULIPs have a 5-year lock-in and tax exemption depends on conditions.
  5. Active funds chase alpha at higher cost. Index funds replicate the benchmark at lower cost.
  6. An ETF trades all day on the exchange with brokerage and bid-ask costs and is often illiquid in India. An index fund is bought or redeemed with the AMC at end-of-day NAV.
  7. Real estate is concentrated, lumpy and illiquid. REITs (commercial property) and InvITs (infrastructure) diversify, are professionally managed, and are listed.
  8. A bank FD has a pre-defined return and DICGC cover of ₹5 lakh. An FMP is not guaranteed and is poorly liquid despite listing. Pre-maturity liquidity in debt instruments is poor.
  9. Index futures and options horizon is up to 3 months (longer-dated options exist). An option buyer's maximum loss is the premium. Index funds are for the long term.
  10. Company deposits usually pay slightly more than the same company's debentures. Premature repayment after 6 months has interest recalculated at 1 percentage point below the rate for the period actually run.
  11. Market-linked retirement products (NPS, ELSS) carry risk that depends on the option chosen. Non-market products (PPF) carry low principal risk. Both are illiquid by design.
  12. Personal accident insurance covers accidental death and disability only, as top-up cover. Life insurance covers death from covered causes and is essential for earners with dependants.

Make it stickPriya retires in 1992 with ₹1 crore at 40:60 equity:debt. The flat 7.8% assumption suggests ₹4.21 lakh a year (a 4.21% withdrawal rate), but actual phase returns of 5.2%, 9.8% and 8.4% drain the corpus by age 85. At 3.5% the same corpus lasts to 89 with ₹64 lakh left.
  1. Set a zero date and decide end-of-year vs beginning-of-year (type 0 or 1) before any PV, FV or PMT.
  2. Growing contributions: build a year-by-year table and FV each contribution to the target date (FV(rate, years left, , −amount)), then sum.
  3. Portfolio drift: track debt and equity separately (opening + return + additions) and compute % allocations each year.
  4. Retirement corpus: FV of today's expenses at inflation to the retirement date, then PV at the real rate for the retirement years (type 1 if paid at the start).
  5. Education goal: FV each year's cost at education inflation. Money set aside for later years can be discounted at the set-aside rate.
  6. Blended return = Σ(weight × return). Real return = (1+blended)/(1+inflation) − 1. First-year pension = PMT(real, years, −corpus, , 1).

  11. For retirement income, government-scheme options include SCSS (₹30 lakh cap). Deterministic outputs should be stress-tested for sequence risk.

Bonus strippingBuying just before a bonus record date and selling the originals at a loss. That loss is disallowed and added to the bonus units' cost.

MutationChanging the name in revenue records for fiscal purposes. It is not proof of title.
