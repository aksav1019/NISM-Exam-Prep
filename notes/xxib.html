Portfolio Managers

Run other people's crores like a fiduciary: rules, risk, returns and the maths that proves you earned your fee.

A Portfolio Management Service (PMS) is like hiring a personal chef for your money instead of eating at a buffet (a mutual fund). The PMS firm builds and runs a portfolio in your own name, for a minimum ticket of ₹50 lakh, and can be discretionary (chef decides the menu), non-discretionary (you decide, chef cooks) or advisory (chef only suggests). Because these managers hold huge sums in a fiduciary capacity, SEBI wants the people at the top, the Principal Officer and anyone with fund-management decision power, to prove they know investments, risk, regulation and performance measurement. This exam is that proof.

👥 Who needs it

  • Principal Officer of a SEBI-registered portfolio manager (must hold the relevant NISM certification specified by SEBI, plus qualification and 5 years' experience)
  • Any PMS employee with decision-making authority related to fund management (same qualification, experience and certification as the Principal Officer)
  • PMS distributors, who must hold valid certification as specified by SEBI at all times (Code of Conduct for PMS Distributors) and register with APMI
  • Other employees of PMS firms who want the common minimum knowledge benchmark the exam sets

🧭 Exam strategy

  • Budget time: about 80 minutes for the 90 MCQs and 90 minutes for the 30 caselet sub-questions, plus 10 minutes to review.
  • Caselet sub-questions are worth 2 marks each (60 marks in total). Read the case once, note all numbers, then solve the sub-questions in order because they often build on each other.
  • Keep Excel open: use IRR/XIRR for MWRR, YIELD/PRICE/DURATION for bonds, and SQRT/NORM.S.INV checks for VaR.
  • Negative marking is 25%: guess only when you can eliminate at least two options (a wrong caselet answer costs 0.5 mark).
  • Chapters 7, 8, 11, 17, 18, 19 and 20 carry 80 of 150 marks. Master them first.
  • Regulatory questions turn on exact numbers and words ('shall not', 'except advisory'). Learn the number table cold.
  • For fee caselets, follow the workbook order: gross value, minus expenses and fixed fee, compare with hurdle and high water mark, then apply performance fee, then exit load.
  • When asked Sharpe vs Treynor, check how diversified the investor's total wealth is.
  • Watch units: annual vs daily σ (√250), percent vs decimal, and semi-annual YTM in modified duration.

Chapter 1 · Weight: 3 marks

Investments

Why people invest, how investing differs from saving and speculation, and how the required return is built: real risk-free rate, then inflation, then a risk premium. It also maps the investment universe and the channels (direct, advisers, PMS, funds) for getting there.

Make it stickLending a friend ₹1,000 and asking for ₹1,020 back is the pure rate of interest at work. Ask for more because prices will rise, and more again because your friend is unreliable, and you've just built the required rate of return: real rate, then inflation, then risk premium.
  1. Saving = income minus spending; investment = committing savings for a period expecting a higher amount back
  2. Speculation: taking risk not commensurate with return, with minimal research on true value; it is not defined only by short holding period
  3. Investment objectives: capital preservation, capital appreciation, current income, tax saving; objectives must be stated in terms of risk AND return
  4. Required return = real risk-free rate, plus compensation for expected inflation, plus a risk premium
  5. Nominal rate = (1 + real rate) x (1 + expected inflation) - 1, e.g. (1.02 x 1.06) - 1 = 8.12%
  6. Real risk-free rate depends on the desire for current consumption (subjective) and investment opportunities / real growth of the economy (objective)
  7. Risk is 'known uncertainty': dispersion around expected return; uncertainty is when causal factors are unknown
  8. Business risk comes from sales volatility and operating leverage; financial risk comes from debt (financial leverage)
  9. Liquidity risk: difficulty converting to cash near economic worth (T-bills ≈ none; art = high)
  10. Other risks: exchange rate, political (country), geopolitical, regulatory
  11. Risk and return are positively related, though not linearly
  12. Financial investments can be in security form (tradable: shares, bonds) or non-security form (FDs, insurance: no secondary market)

Workbook example2% real + 6% inflation = 8.12% nominal
Required return componentsReal RFR + inflation premium + risk premium

Chapter 2 · Weight: 2 marks

Introduction to Securities Markets

How the primary market raises fresh capital (IPO, FPO, rights, private placement, QIP, OFS) and how the secondary market gives liquidity, with clearing corporations guaranteeing exchange trades. It also lists the intermediaries and participants.

Make it stickThe primary market is the car showroom where new cars (shares) are sold by the maker. The secondary market is the used-car market where owners sell to each other. The clearing corporation is the escrow agent who makes sure every buyer gets the car and every seller gets paid.
  1. Primary market: issuers raise fresh capital; secondary market: investors trade among themselves, providing liquidity
  2. Private placement under the Companies Act 2013: offer to less than 200 persons
  3. QIP: private placement by a listed company to Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs)
  4. OFS: existing shareholders sell; no increase in share capital; proceeds go to the sellers, not the company
  5. Bonus issue: no consideration; made from free reserves; cannot be made if the company has defaulted on debt or FD payments; cannot be withdrawn once announced
  6. Anchor investor: a QIB applying for ₹10 crore or more in a book-built public issue (introduced 2009)
  7. ADRs list in the USA, GDRs list outside the USA, IDRs are issued in India on foreign shares
  8. Clearing corporation provides full novation: buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer
  9. Two depositories: NSDL and CDSL; Depository Participants are their agents
  10. Custodians hold funds and securities, mainly for institutions and FPIs, and settle trades
Private placementOffer to < 200 persons
Anchor investor minimum₹10 crore application
DepositoriesNSDL and CDSL

⚠️ TrapAn OFS is a primary-market route but a secondary-market transaction: the company gets no money.

Chapter 3 · Weight: 5 marks

Investing in Stocks

Equity as an asset class, its risks, and how analysts value shares: DCF (FCFF/FCFE), dividend discount, and relative multiples like P/E, PEG, P/S and EV/EBITDA. It also touches technical analysis and corporate governance.

Make it stickValuing a stock is like valuing a mango orchard: you can count the future mangoes and discount them (DCF), or check what similar orchards sold for per tree (multiples). A PEG below 1 is like an orchard priced cheaply for how fast it is growing.
  1. Equity risks: market, sector-specific, company-specific, liquidity, currency
  2. Three valuation approaches: discounted cash flow, asset-based and relative (multiple-based)
  3. FCFF is discounted at WACC to value the firm; FCFE is discounted at cost of equity to value equity
  4. Cost of equity (CAPM): Ke = Rf + β(Rm - Rf), where Rf is usually the 10-year government bond yield
  5. WACC = Ke x We + Kd x (1 - tax) x Wd
  6. Sustainable growth proxy = retention ratio x ROE = (1 - payout) x ROE
  7. Dividend discount model: P = D1 / (k - g), with D1 = D0 x (1 + g)
  8. Forward P/E = payout / (k - g): rises with growth, falls with required return
  9. PEG = P/E ÷ EPS growth; PEG < 1 suggests undervalued, > 1 overvalued
  10. EV = market cap of equity + market value of debt - excess cash
  11. EVA = NOPAT - (invested capital x cost of capital); MVA = market value minus capital contributed
  12. Technical analysis assumes all information is in price and volume; moving averages are the most popular indicator

⚠️ TrapUse D1 (next year's dividend), not D0, in the dividend discount model. Discounting FCFE at WACC is wrong.

Chapter 4 · Weight: 5 marks

Investing in Fixed Income Securities

Bond features, credit ratings, yield measures and the maths of bond price volatility: duration, modified duration and convexity. Expect Excel-style YIELD/PRICE/DURATION caselets.

Make it stickA bond is a see-saw: rates go up, prices go down. A long-maturity, low-coupon bond sits far from the pivot, so it swings the most. A zero-coupon bond sits at the very end of the plank.
  1. Government securities carry practically no default risk, hence 'gilt-edged'
  2. Current yield = annual coupon ÷ current market price
  3. YTM assumes all coupons are reinvested at the YTM; reinvestment risk is the risk this fails
  4. Bond prices and interest rates move inversely
  5. Lower coupon means higher price volatility; zero-coupon bonds show the largest % change
  6. Longer maturity means higher volatility, but it increases at a diminishing rate
  7. Price changes are asymmetric: a fall in yield raises price more than an equal rise lowers it (convexity)
  8. Macaulay duration (years) = weighted average time to recover the price in PV terms; it is ≤ maturity and equals maturity only for zero-coupon bonds
  9. Modified duration = Macaulay duration ÷ (1 + YTM per period)
  10. % price change ≈ - modified duration x change in yield (decimal)
  11. Call option lets the issuer retire bonds early; put option lets holders sell back to the issuer
  12. Excel: YIELD, PRICE and DURATION functions; Indian day count is 30/360 (basis 0)

Chapter 5 · Weight: 5 marks

Derivatives

Forwards, futures, options and swaps, how exchange-traded markets manage counterparty risk, and the specific rules for portfolio managers using derivatives.

Make it stickA futures contract is a restaurant booking with a cancellation fee collected upfront (margin). The clearing corporation is the restaurant manager who guarantees your table even if another diner fails to show.
  1. Forwards are OTC and customised; futures are standardised and exchange-traded
  2. Options give the buyer a right (not an obligation); the buyer pays a premium to the seller
  3. Swaps exchange cash flows, e.g. fixed for floating interest
  4. Counterparty risk in futures is mitigated by the clearing corporation
  5. Derivatives are a zero-sum game (ignoring taxes and transaction costs)
  6. SEBI mandates physical settlement for individual stock and commodity derivatives; index derivatives are cash-settled
  7. Initial margin = SPAN margin + Extreme Loss Margin; it should cover losses in 99% of cases; option buyers pay premium margin
  8. Open interest = outstanding unsettled contracts; it is not trading volume
  9. Commodity options in India devolve into commodity futures
  10. PMS may use derivatives (including for hedging and rebalancing) via recognised stock exchanges, on terms in the PMS agreement
  11. A client's total derivatives exposure must not exceed the portfolio funds placed with the PM; PMs cannot leverage client portfolios for derivatives
Initial marginSPAN + ELM, covers 99% of cases
PMS derivatives capExposure ≤ client's portfolio funds
SettlementStock and commodity derivatives physical; index derivatives cash

Chapter 6 · Weight: 5 marks

Mutual Fund

The trust-based structure of Indian mutual funds (sponsor, trustees, AMC, custodian, RTA), how NAV and pricing work, factsheets, and how performance must be advertised.

Make it stickA mutual fund is a shared tiffin service: everyone chips in, the cook (AMC) makes the meal, the trustees taste-check it on your behalf, and the custodian keeps the pantry locked.
  1. Mutual funds are constituted as trusts under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882
  2. Sponsor creates the trust; trustees protect unit-holders; AMC manages day-to-day; custodian (appointed by trustees) holds assets
  3. The AMC is appointed by the sponsor or trustees with SEBI approval
  4. NAV = (market value of assets + income - expenses) ÷ units outstanding, declared daily
  5. Open-ended schemes transact at NAV-related prices; close-ended units trade on exchanges at market price
  6. Factsheet is voluntary (market practice) but SEBI disclosure rules apply to it
  7. Schemes older than 3 years advertise CAGR for 1, 3, 5 years and since inception, plus point-to-point value of ₹10,000
  8. Liquid/money market schemes may show simple annualised yields for 7, 15 and 30 days
  9. Compliance officer reports directly to the AMC head and reports non-compliance immediately to trustees

Custodian appointed byTrustees

Chapter 7 · Weight: 10 marks

Role of Portfolio Managers

Who a portfolio manager is, the three service types, registration requirements under SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations 2020, and the core duties and don'ts. This is the regulatory backbone of the exam.

Make it stickThink of a discretionary PMS as a valet who drives your car without asking, a non-discretionary PMS as a driver who goes only where you say, and advisory as a friend in the passenger seat giving directions while you drive.
  1. A portfolio manager must be a body corporate (includes LLPs; excludes co-operative societies) registered with SEBI
  2. First PMS regulations came in January 1993, even before the mutual fund regulations
  3. Discretionary: manager decides (any degree of discretion); non-discretionary: client decides, manager executes; advisory: manager advises, client decides and executes
  4. Net worth requirement: ₹5 crore, maintained at all times (not applicable to co-investment PM)
  5. Principal Officer: professional qualification (finance, law, accountancy, business management, NISM 1-year PGP or CFA charter), plus 5 years' experience (at least 2 in portfolio management, investment advisory or fund management), plus NISM certification
  6. At least one more employee: graduate with 2 years' experience; anyone with fund-management decision authority needs the Principal Officer's qualifications
  7. Minimum investment ₹50 lakh per client (not for accredited investors or co-investment PM)
  8. Client funds in a separate account in a Scheduled Commercial Bank; each client's securities segregated
  9. Cannot borrow funds/securities for clients, cannot lend securities except as permitted, cannot charge distribution fees on direct-plan MF units, cannot leverage for derivatives
  10. Cannot invest in portfolios managed by another PM, or on the advice of any other entity; no bill discounting, badla or lending
  11. Pooled trades must be allocated pro rata at the weighted average price of the day, with no open positions
  12. Off-market transfers allowed only for own settlement, margin, unlisted dealings, specific client consent, or as SEBI specifies
  13. Exit load cap: 3% (year 1), 2% (year 2), 1% (year 3), nil after 3 years; no lock-in allowed
  14. Custodian mandatory except for advisory-only PMs (and co-investment PMs); records preserved for 5 accounting years
  15. Compliance officer cannot be the Principal Officer (except for co-investment PMs) and must report non-compliance to SEBI independently

Chapter 8 · Weight: 10 marks

Operational Aspects of Portfolio Managers

Who can invest, what the Disclosure Document must contain, onboarding and KYC, the client agreement, grievance redressal and ODR, reporting to SEBI and FIU, and the PMS fee mechanics (high water mark, hurdle rate). Caselets on performance fee are common.

Make it stickA high water mark is like a mountain climber's best altitude: the guide (manager) gets a bonus only for climbing above the highest point already reached, not for re-climbing ground lost in a fall.
  1. Eligible investors (min ₹50 lakh): individuals, NRIs (per RBI), HUFs, proprietorships, AOPs, partnership firms, LLPs, trusts, body corporates
  2. Disclosure Document must be given with the account opening form before or at the time of signing; the old '2 days in advance' rule was dropped in 2020
  3. Simplified Disclosure Document format via SEBI circular of 9 September 2025: static Part I and dynamic Part II (performance for last 3 years using TWRR, audit observations of 3 years)
  4. Dealing room: recorded lines only, no mobile phones, restricted internet and access
  5. PMs with AUM of ₹1,000 crore or more need an automated order management and allocation system with minimal manual intervention
  6. Uniform KYC since 1 January 2012 via KRA; CKYCR upload for individual accounts opened from 1 August 2016
  7. Client must separately write or type that they understood the fee structure; fee calculation tool and MITC document mandatory
  8. Direct onboarding option must be offered with no charges except statutory charges (not for co-investment PMS)
  9. Client may invest cash, securities or both worth at least ₹50 lakh; joint-holding benefits go to the first holder
  10. Dispute resolution (Reg. 22A, from 3 July 2023): mediation, conciliation or arbitration via SMART ODR; pre-conciliation and conciliation are free for investors
  11. Suspicious transactions must be reported to FIU-IND within 7 working days
  12. No upfront fees; brokerage at actuals; operating expenses (excluding brokerage) capped at 0.50% p.a. of average daily AUM
  13. Performance fee is charged only on gains above the high water mark and, usually, above a hurdle rate

Chapter 9 · Weight: 10 marks

Portfolio Management Process

The planning, execution and feedback loop: Investment Policy Statement, objectives and constraints, investor profiling (BB&K personalities, life-cycle phases), benchmarking, strategic vs tactical asset allocation and rebalancing.

Make it stickThe IPS is the GPS route for a road trip: destination (objectives), rules of the road (constraints) and how you'll judge the driver (benchmark). SAA is the planned route; TAA is a quick detour to dodge a traffic jam.
  1. Steps: (1) policy statement, (2) study current conditions and forecast, (3) construct the portfolio, (4) measure and evaluate performance
  2. IPS purposes: realistic expectations, effective decisions, a framework for evaluating the manager, and protection against inappropriate or unethical decisions
  3. Objectives are framed in risk, return and liquidity; constraints are liquidity, time horizon, tax, regulatory and unique preferences
  4. Emergency cash: usually 2 to 3 months' spending
  5. LRS: resident individuals can remit up to USD 2,50,000 per financial year
  6. BB&K personalities: Adventurer (confident, impetuous), Celebrity (anxious, impetuous), Individualist (confident, careful), Guardian (anxious, careful), Straight Arrow (centre)
  7. Life-cycle phases: accumulation, consolidation, spending, gifting
  8. Investment approaches are tagged to exactly one strategy (equity, debt, hybrid, multi-asset); APMI prescribes at most 3 benchmarks per strategy
  9. Changing an IA's strategy or benchmark tag requires offering existing clients a no-exit-load exit; past track record cannot be used afterwards
  10. SAA = long-term policy mix ('time in the market'); TAA = short-term deviation ('timing the market')
  11. Asset allocation explains about 40% of variation in returns across funds and about 90% of a single fund's return variation over time (Brinson, Hood and Beebower)
  12. Rebalancing costs: transaction costs and taxes; illiquid assets are harder to rebalance

Chapter 10 · Weight: 5 marks

Taxation

Residential status (individual, HUF, company), scope of total income, capital gains vs business income, holding-period rules, dividend and interest taxation, buy-back rules, and the Section 9A safe harbour for offshore funds managed from India.

Make it stickYour tax passport is stamped by days, not by citizenship: stay 182 days and you're resident. An Indian citizen who escapes tax everywhere but earns over ₹15 lakh in India is pulled back as a 'deemed resident' (NOR).
  1. Resident if in India 182 days or more, OR 60 days (and 365 days in the preceding 4 years); 60 becomes 120 days for citizens/PIOs with Indian income above ₹15 lakh
  2. Deemed resident: Indian citizen with Indian income above ₹15 lakh and not liable to tax elsewhere; always treated as NOR
  3. NOR if non-resident in 9 of 10 preceding years, or in India 729 days or less in 7 preceding years
  4. Indian company is always resident; a foreign company is resident if its POEM is in India (CBDT guidelines apply above ₹50 crore turnover)
  5. Firm/AOP/HUF is resident unless control and management is wholly outside India
  6. A PMS client is taxed as if investing directly in the underlying securities
  7. Capital gains indicators: intention of appreciation, low frequency, long holding, shown as investments, own funds
  8. Listed securities, equity MF units, UTI units, zero-coupon bonds: long term if held more than 12 months
  9. Unlisted shares and other securities: long term if held more than 24 months
  10. Specified MF (≤35% in Indian equity, acquired on/after 1 April 2023) and market-linked debentures: always short term
  11. Interest deduction against dividend income restricted to 20% of gross dividend; TDS 10% on dividends to residents above ₹10,000
  12. Buy-back proceeds from 1 October 2024 are taxed as dividend for the shareholder; cost of acquisition becomes a capital loss
  13. Section 9A: fund ≥ 25 members, single member ≤ 10%, top 10 or fewer < 50%, residents ≤ 5% of corpus, ≤ 20% in any entity, monthly average corpus ≥ ₹100 crore; manager's profit share ≤ 20%

Chapter 11 · Weight: 10 marks

Regulatory, Governance and Ethical Aspects of Portfolio Managers

PMLA record-keeping, insider-trading code for intermediaries, PFUTP prohibitions, the 2020 PMS Regulations summary (permitted investments, reports, related-party limits, code of conduct), Eligible Fund Managers, distributor code, research objectivity, soft dollars, investor charter and cyber security.

Make it stickThe regulations are the referee's rulebook. PMLA watches the money's origin, the insider rules guard secrets behind a Chinese wall, PFUTP blows the whistle on market tricks, and the PMS rules make sure the manager never puts his own goals ahead of the client's.
  1. PMLA in force from 1 July 2005: report cash transactions above ₹10 lakh, or integrally connected series exceeding ₹10 lakh in a month
  2. PMLA records: 10 years (rules) for prescribed transactions; Section 12 records 5 years from transaction or end of relationship
  3. Insider trading code: pre-cleared trades executed within 7 trading days; contra trade barred for at least 6 months; profits go to the IPEF
  4. 'Material financial relationship': payments of at least 25% of the designated person's annual income in the past 12 months
  5. Discretionary PMs invest only in listed/traded securities, money market instruments, MF units and SEBI-specified securities
  6. Non-discretionary and advisory PMs may invest up to 25% of AUM in unlisted securities; up to 100% for large value accredited investors
  7. MF investments only through direct plans
  8. Reports to clients at intervals not exceeding 3 months, including commission paid to distributors
  9. Auditor's deficiencies must be rectified within 2 months of the report
  10. Associate/related-party limits: 15% single associate (equity, or debt/hybrid), 25% across associates, 30% overall (not applicable to MF investments)
  11. Regulatory sandbox exemption up to 12 months
  12. Code of conduct: PM must obtain in writing the client's interests in bodies that could give UPSI; media advice requires disclosure of positions
  13. Eligible Fund Managers (Chapter III) are exempt from HWM, fiduciary, ₹50 lakh minimum etc.; they file quarterly reports to SEBI
  14. PMS distributors must register with APMI and never assure returns
  15. Soft-dollar arrangements should be avoided; ROS requires analyst pay not linked to investment banking
  16. CSCRF: PMs with AUM above ₹3,000 crore are mid-size REs; ≤ ₹3,000 crore are self-certification REs; fewer than 100 clients exempt from M-SOC; cyber incidents reported within 6 hours (email) and 24 hours (portal)

Chapter 12 · Weight: 5 marks

Introduction to Indices

What indices are for, and the four weighting schemes (price, value/free-float, equal, fundamental), divisor adjustments, index methodologies (Sensex, NIFTY 50), bond indices and hybrid composites.

Make it stickA price-weighted index is a class where the tallest kid's mood decides the class photo. A value-weighted index lets the biggest kids matter most. An equal-weighted one gives everyone the same say.
  1. Uses: market gauge, benchmark, basis for index funds, proxy for the market portfolio (beta)
  2. Dow Jones Average (1884) was the first index and is price-weighted
  3. Price-weighted: sum of prices ÷ divisor; high-priced stocks dominate; the divisor is adjusted for splits (downward bias for growth stocks that split)
  4. Value-weighted: weights by (free-float) market cap; auto-adjusts for splits; large caps dominate
  5. Sensex moved to free float on 1 September 2003; NIFTY 50 on 26 June 2009
  6. Free float excludes promoter, government-promoter, FDI, strategic, cross-holdings, ESOP trust and locked-in shares
  7. Equal-weighted: arithmetic average of returns; no size bias but high turnover and rebalancing cost
  8. Fundamental-weighted: weights by sales, book value, dividends etc.; counters overweighting of overvalued stocks
  9. NIFTY 50 eligibility: 100% trading frequency over 6 months; impact cost ≤ 0.50% for a ₹10 crore portfolio in 90% of observations; free-float cap at least 1.5x the smallest constituent
  10. Sensex: 30 companies from S&P BSE 100; 6-month listing history
  11. NIFTY G-Sec indices: 6 duration buckets; bonds above ₹5,000 crore outstanding; rebalanced monthly
  12. NIFTY 50 Hybrid Composite Debt indices blend NIFTY 50 TR with debt (e.g. 70:30, 65:35, 50:50, 15:85); reset monthly

Chapter 13 · Weight: 5 marks

Concept of Informational Efficiency

Operational vs informational efficiency, random walk, Fama's three forms of EMH, anomalies (January, size, value) and what efficiency means for technical analysis, fundamental analysis and index funds.

Make it stickIn an efficient market, finding a ₹500 note on a crowded street is nearly impossible: someone else picked it up seconds ago. The paradox is that the street stays clean only because so many people keep looking.
  1. Operational efficiency = low transaction costs; informational efficiency = prices fully reflect information
  2. Efficient price is an unbiased estimate of value; deviations are random
  3. Fama (1970) formalised EMH as a fair game model
  4. Weak form: past price/volume data reflected, so technical analysis is useless
  5. Semi-strong form: all public information reflected, so fundamental analysis on public data cannot earn abnormal returns
  6. Strong form: public plus private (insider) information reflected
  7. Anomalies: January effect (tax-loss selling), size effect (small firms earn more), value effect (high book-to-price earns more)
  8. Internal contradiction: markets become efficient only because participants believe they are not
  9. Efficiency supports index funds: be with the market and minimise transaction cost and tax

Chapter 14 · Weight: 5 marks

Behavioural Finance

Why real investors depart from rational models: bounded rationality, prospect theory, emotional biases vs cognitive errors, fusion investing and the psychology of bubbles and crashes.

Make it stickHolding a losing stock 'until it comes back' while quickly selling a winner to 'lock in profit' is the disposition effect: a ₹1,000 loss hurts more than a ₹1,000 gain pleases.
  1. Standard finance assumes rational, risk-averse, self-interested investors with full information
  2. Kahneman (2002) and Thaler (2017) Nobel prizes brought behavioural finance forward
  3. Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon): people 'satisfice' rather than optimise
  4. Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979): reference point; risk-averse in gains, risk-seeking in losses; losses hurt more
  5. Emotional biases: loss aversion, stereotyping, overconfidence, endowment, status quo / regret aversion
  6. Cognitive errors: mental accounting (Thaler), framing, anchoring
  7. Disposition effect (Shefrin and Statman, 1985): sell winners early, hold losers too long
  8. Regret from errors of commission is more intense than from omission, which drives status quo bias
  9. Fusion investing combines value/growth fundamentals with momentum: value screen, then quality (e.g. Piotroski score 0 to 9), then momentum
  10. Bubbles are fuelled by liquidity, celebrity status, momentum (herding) and illusion of control

Introduction to Modern Portfolio Theory

Markowitz's mean-variance framework: expected return and variance of a security and a portfolio, covariance and correlation, the efficient frontier, utility and estimation issues.

Make it stickMixing an umbrella seller with an ice-cream seller smooths your income whatever the weather. That's negative correlation reducing portfolio risk without giving up average return.
  1. Markowitz 'Portfolio Selection' (Journal of Finance, 1952); Nobel 1990
  2. Investors maximise return for a given risk and decide solely on expected return and risk
  3. Expected return = Σ probability x return; portfolio return = Σ weight x return
  4. Portfolio variance depends on weights, individual risks and covariances (correlations)
  5. Two assets: σp² = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2w1w2ρσ1σ2
  6. Covariance = ρ x σ1 x σ2; correlation ranges from -1 to +1
  7. If ρ < 1 then portfolio σ < weighted average σ (diversification); ρ = -1 can give zero risk
  8. Efficient frontier: highest return for each risk level (umbrella-shaped curve)
  9. Utility U = E(r) - ½Aσ²; invest if utility exceeds the risk-free rate
  10. Number of correlation estimates = (n² - n)/2, e.g. 1,225 for 50 securities
  11. Inputs: expected returns, standard deviations and the correlation matrix; errors are estimation risk

Introduction to Capital Market Theory

Adding a risk-free asset turns the efficient frontier into the Capital Market Line; the market portfolio removes unsystematic risk; CAPM and the Security Market Line price systematic risk; APT extends to multiple factors.

Make it stickWith a risk-free asset you only need one risky 'super-portfolio' M. A cautious investor mixes M with T-bills, an aggressive one borrows to buy more M. Everyone just turns the risk dial on the same line.
  1. CAPM developed by Sharpe (1964), Treynor (1962), Lintner (1965) and Mossin (1966)
  2. Assumptions include borrowing/lending at Rf, homogeneous expectations, one-period horizon, no taxes or costs, equilibrium
  3. Risk-free asset: zero SD and zero correlation with risky assets
  4. CML: E(Rp) = Rf + (Rm - Rf) x σp/σm; portfolios on the CML are perfectly correlated with M
  5. Lending portfolios lie left of M, borrowing portfolios right of M
  6. Market portfolio M contains all risky assets in market-value proportions, so its correlation with a completely diversified portfolio is +1
  7. Diversification benefits largely end after about 20 to 30 securities
  8. CAPM: E(Ri) = Rf + βi (E(Rm) - Rf); β = Cov(i,m)/σm²; market beta = 1
  9. SML plots return against beta; above SML = undervalued, below = overvalued
  10. Beta is unstable for single stocks but stable for portfolios (over 50 stocks, over 26 weeks); betas regress toward 1
  11. APT (Stephen Ross): multiple factor betas; does not need a market portfolio or normal returns

Chapter 17 · Weight: 10 marks

Risk

The risk management process, the zoo of risks (market, non-market, liquidity, operational, regulatory, legal, geopolitical, currency, country, concentration, pandemic) and how to measure them: beta, VaR (parametric, historical, Monte Carlo), stress tests, bid-ask liquidity cost and credit risk (PD, EAD, LGD).

Make it stickVaR is a weather forecast for losses: '95% of days, you won't lose more than ₹1.2 million.' It says nothing about how bad the other 5% storms can be, which is why stress tests exist.
  1. Process: set objectives, identify, analyse, evaluate (severity and frequency), treat (tolerate, mitigate, transfer, terminate), control and monitor
  2. Market (systematic) risk is hedged with derivatives; non-market risk is diversified away
  3. Company-specific risk = business risk + financial risk
  4. Diversification benefit flattens after about 30 to 35 stocks
  5. Regulatory risk is a part of legal risk; legal risk relates to enforceability of contracts
  6. VaR: maximum loss over a period, at a confidence level, in normal market conditions
  7. Parametric VaR = [E(daily return) x n - z x σdaily x √n] x portfolio value; 250 or 252 trading days
  8. One-tailed z: 1.282 (90%), 1.645 (95%), 2.33 (99%); 1.96 is for a two-sided 95% range
  9. Historical simulation VaR is usually lower than parametric; Monte Carlo (~10,000 runs) handles complex portfolios and any distribution
  10. VaR limitations: subjectivity; underestimates extreme losses and gains
  11. Sensitivity measures: beta (equity), duration (bonds), delta/gamma/vega (options); stress test = extreme scenario
  12. Liquidity cost = (ask - bid)/mid x mid value; liquidity-adjusted VaR = VaR + cost of unwinding
  13. Credit risk: probability of default, exposure at default, loss given default; four Cs: capacity, collateral, covenants, capital

Equity Portfolio Management Strategies

Passive (buy and hold, indexing by full replication or sampling) vs active (market timing, sector rotation), the fundamental law of active management, smart beta and factor models, momentum, growth/value/blend styles, ESG screening, core-satellite, alpha-beta separation, derivatives and global investing.

Make it stickCore-satellite is a thali: the rice and dal (70 to 80% passive core) keep you fed cheaply, while the small spicy pickles (active satellites) add the flavour, and the cost.
  1. Passive aims to match the benchmark; active aims to beat it on a risk-adjusted basis at higher cost
  2. Full replication suits small, liquid indices (NIFTY 50, Sensex); sampling suits large indices (NIFTY 500)
  3. Market timers raise beta or shift to stocks when expecting a rise; sector rotation is a top-down strategy
  4. Fundamental law (Grinold and Kahn, 1989): IR = IC x √Breadth
  5. IC = 2 x (right predictions ÷ total) - 1; ranges from -1 to +1; IC = 0 is a coin toss
  6. Breadth = independent forecasts per period x number of periods; doubling breadth raises IR by √2 (1.414x)
  7. Smart beta: rule-based like passive, factor choice like active (quality, value, low volatility, momentum)
  8. NSE multi-factor indices (July 2017): Alpha Low-Vol 30, Quality Low-Vol 30, Alpha Quality Low-Vol 30, Alpha Quality Value Low-Vol 30
  9. Factor models: macroeconomic, fundamental, statistical
  10. Growth: high earnings growth, low payout, high valuation; value: low P/E, P/B, P/S, PEG, high dividend yield
  11. SRI screens: negative (exclude sin stocks), positive (rank on ESG), best-in-class (best per sector)
  12. Core-satellite: about 70 to 80% core (passive, minimise tracking error), rest satellite (active)
  13. Alpha-beta separation / portable alpha: beta from passive exposure, alpha from market-neutral long-short
  14. Protective put caps downside at the premium; selling index futures hedges a diversified portfolio
  15. Market cap to GDP: below 50% undervalued; 50 to 75% moderately undervalued; 75 to 90% fair; 90 to 110% moderately overvalued; above 110% overvalued
  16. Home bias reasons: familiarity, optimism, risks, predictability; costs: concentration and missed diversification

Chapter 19 · Weight: 15 marks

Fixed Income Portfolio Management Strategies

Passive bond strategies (buy and hold, indexing, immunization), active strategies (duration calls, barbell/bullet, floaters, maturity extension, roll-down, convexity, credit and spread analysis), global bonds and derivatives (IRS, swaptions, futures, forwards, CDS, currency swaps).

Make it stickImmunization is balancing a see-saw: if rates fall, bond prices rise but reinvested coupons earn less; if rates rise, the reverse. Set the duration equal to your horizon and the two sides cancel out.
  1. Passive strategies: buy and hold, indexing, immunization
  2. Buy and hold locks yield and removes price risk but not reinvestment risk
  3. Tracking error causes: costs, similar-not-same securities, weight differences, illiquid securities, concentration limits, cash holdings
  4. Benchmark choice: risk-free long-term = G-Sec index; short-term cash = liquid index; income = long duration + aggregate; low risk = low duration + high credit quality; inflation = floating-rate benchmark
  5. Immunization: Macaulay duration of the portfolio = liability horizon; zero-coupon bond with matching maturity is the ideal
  6. Immunization needs periodic re-balancing as rates change, so it is not as passive as buy and hold
  7. Expect rates up: cut duration; expect rates down: raise duration
  8. Barbell (e.g. 40% short / 20% mid / 40% long) when the rate direction is uncertain; durations above 7 years long, 3 to 7 mid, below 3 short
  9. Floater's duration is only the time to the next reset
  10. Roll-down: on an upward-sloping curve, yield falls as maturity shortens, so price rises
  11. Positive convexity: price rises more and falls less than duration predicts
  12. Yield curve 3 S's: shift, slope, shape
  13. Credit spreads widen in recessions and narrow in expansions; term spread = long yield minus short yield
  14. Credit research uses the four Cs to estimate PD and LGD; credit ratings are mandatory before issuing bonds or CP in India
  15. CDS = insurance against a credit event; swaption = option to enter an IRS; cross-currency swap exchanges floating payments in two currencies

Performance Measurement and Evaluation of Portfolio Managers

Every return measure (HPR, TWRR, MWRR/IRR, AM vs GM, gross vs net, pre vs post tax, CAGR, cash drag, alpha), risk measures (SD, semi-variance, beta, tracking error), risk-adjusted ratios (Sharpe, Treynor, Sortino, Information Ratio, M²), benchmarking, attribution analysis, SEBI reporting rules and GIPS.

Make it stickTWRR grades the driver; MWRR grades the trip. If the passenger keeps adding fuel just before uphill stretches, the trip looks worse, but that's not the driver's fault. So SEBI judges managers on TWRR.
  1. HPR = (Income + End - Begin) ÷ Begin
  2. TWRR chain-links sub-period returns, removes the effect of client cash flows; mandated by GIPS and SEBI for PM performance
  3. Sub-period return = (EMV - BMV - contribution + withdrawal) ÷ (BMV + contribution - withdrawal)
  4. MWRR = IRR, depends on timing of cash flows
  5. Investors get XIRR with min, max and median XIRR across investors; IA performance uses TWRR with the benchmark's trailing return
  6. Geometric mean ≤ arithmetic mean (equal only if all returns are equal)
  7. Post-tax = pre-tax x (1 - t); pre-tax = post-tax ÷ (1 - t)
  8. Cash drag: include cash and liquid funds in return calculations
  9. Performance must be net of all fees and expenses (including taxes); marketing must carry a 'not verified by SEBI' disclaimer
  10. Jensen alpha = Rp - [Rf + β(Rm - Rf)]
  11. Beta = Cov(p,m)/Var(m) = ρ x σp/σm; portfolio beta is the weighted average
  12. Sharpe = (Rp - Rf)/σp for poorly diversified investors; Treynor = (Rp - Rf)/β for well-diversified investors
  13. Sortino uses downside (semi) deviation; Information Ratio = active return ÷ tracking error
  14. M² = (Rp - Rf) x σm/σp + Rf, compared with Rm
  15. Attribution: allocation Σ Rpi(Wai - Wpi), selection Σ Wpi(Rai - Rpi), interaction Σ(Wai - Wpi)(Rai - Rpi)
  16. APMI prescribes valuation norms and empanels agencies for debt/money market valuation; PaRRVA verifies past risk-return claims
  17. GIPS advertising: 1, 3, 5 year annualised returns; periods under 1 year must not be annualised

Portfolio Rebalancing

Why portfolios drift, the cost-benefit of rebalancing, calendar vs threshold rules, and three dynamic strategies: buy and hold (do nothing), constant mix (do something) and CPPI (floor plus multiplier).

Make it stickCPPI is a trapeze act with a safety net. The net is the floor; the height you climb is the cushion times your courage (multiplier). Fall towards the net and you automatically climb down.
  1. Rebalancing is needed due to market drift and changes in investor circumstances; the IPS should hold a rebalancing policy
  2. Costs: monitoring, transaction costs and taxes on selling appreciated assets; illiquid assets are harder to rebalance
  3. Calendar (time-based) rebalancing is simple; quarterly is popular but ignores how far the mix has drifted
  4. Threshold (corridor) rebalancing: e.g. 60% equity target with 50% and 70% triggers; tighter control but needs monitoring
  5. Higher transaction cost assets get wider corridors; more volatile assets get narrower ones
  6. Buy and hold: do nothing; floor from safe assets; unlimited upside; market risk tends to increase
  7. Constant mix: periodically restore weights (sell winners, buy losers); maintains market risk exposure
  8. CPPI: risky exposure = m x (portfolio value - floor); floor = PV of protected amount at Rf; multipliers usually 3 to 5

  14. IPS is the first step; SAA = time in the market; TAA = timing the market.
  15. BB&K: Adventurer, Celebrity, Individualist, Guardian, Straight Arrow.

  21. Mental accounting, framing, anchoring = cognitive; loss aversion, overconfidence, endowment, status quo = emotional.
  22. σp < weighted average σ whenever ρ < +1.
  23. CML uses σ (efficient portfolios); SML uses β (all securities). Above SML = undervalued.
